*A diamond tennis necklace done right is the most disciplined piece of fine jewelry you will ever own — every stone matched, every link identical, every clasp built to last decades.*

---

### The Work
## What Makes a Tennis Necklace Different from Every Other Piece

A tennis necklace is a continuous line of individually set diamonds running the full circumference of the neck. Every link connects to the next with a hinge, every stone sits in its own prong or bezel, and the whole thing must flex, lie flat, and read as a single unbroken ribbon of light. That is a fabrication challenge that does not exist in a pendant or a pair of studs.

The number that matters most before carat weight or price is the ratio of diamond diameter to link width. If the stones are too small relative to the link, you see metal first and diamonds second. If the stones are too large, the piece looks heavy and the links pull. At Bez Ambar we prototype each weight class before we cut a single stone to order, because the math on paper does not always match the drape on a neck.

Setting style is the second structural decision. Four-prong settings hold each stone at the corners, letting maximum light enter from the sides. Bezel settings wrap a thin wall of gold around each diamond, producing a sleeker, more modern silhouette and protecting the girdle of every stone. Channel settings nestle pairs of stones between two parallel rails — the look is linear and architectural but it limits the stone shapes you can use. None of these is wrong. They are answers to different aesthetic questions.

### The Diamonds
## How the Stones Are Matched and Why It Takes Time

A finished tennis necklace carrying five total carats of round brilliants may contain sixty to eighty individual diamonds. Every one of those stones must be calibrated to the same diameter within a fraction of a millimeter. Color must grade consistently across the full run. Clarity should be uniform enough that no single stone pulls the eye. That matching process is the primary reason custom tennis necklaces take longer and cost more than what you find behind glass at a chain retailer.

We source stones in parcels and sort by eye before any instrument confirms a match. A grading report tells you a stone grades G color, but the human eye in daylight will immediately find the one stone in the run that reads warmer than its neighbors. Matching is a craft, not a software output.

The total carat weight stated for a tennis necklace is cumulative — the sum of all stones. Five carats distributed across seventy-five stones reads very differently from five carats across twenty-five larger stones. The per-stone size changes the visual weight, the price per carat, and the fabrication time. Be precise about which total weight and stone count you are comparing when you evaluate price across vendors.

### The Price
## What Controls Cost and What You Should Spend

Metal, diamond quality, and labor determine the final price of a tennis necklace. On metal: white gold requires rhodium plating to maintain its color, which means eventual replating. Platinum does not. The weight difference between 14k and 18k gold for the same link structure is negligible, but 18k carries more pure gold per gram, affecting both feel and resale. Platinum costs more at fabrication but less over twenty years of ownership when you account for maintenance.

On diamonds: the classic selection for a tennis necklace is round brilliant, G–H color, SI1–SI2 clarity. That combination delivers strong visual impact at a price point that does not require mortgage-level commitment. Moving to F–G color and VS clarity raises cost significantly without a visible difference at wearing distance. Moving below SI2 risks inclusions that interrupt the light return of individual stones, which becomes visible as dark spots when the necklace is worn.

Labor is the line item most buyers overlook. A well-fabricated tennis necklace has two hundred or more solder points, each inspected before the piece ships. The box clasp or safety clasp at the back must pass a tension test, because the consequence of clasp failure on a continuous diamond necklace is losing the entire piece. This is not where you negotiate down.

A quality custom tennis necklace in 18k white gold, round brilliant diamonds, G–H/SI1, three to five total carats, will run between eight thousand and twenty-two thousand dollars depending on stone count and per-stone size. Mass-market versions at lower price points almost always compromise on stone matching or clasp construction. Both compromises show within two years of regular wear.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How long is a standard diamond tennis necklace?**
Standard lengths run sixteen to eighteen inches, which positions the necklace at the collarbone on most adults. We cut each piece to the client's measured neck circumference plus an inch for comfortable drape. A necklace that sits too high will twist; one that sits too low loses the collarbone frame that makes the piece work visually.

**Can a tennis necklace be resized after purchase?**
Yes, within limits. Adding links requires sourcing matched stones, which is straightforward if we made the original piece. Removing links is simpler. The constraint is the clasp position — the clasp should sit at the back center, so adjustments need to be balanced on both sides of the closure.

**What is the difference between a tennis necklace and a riviera necklace?**
A riviera necklace graduates from smaller stones at the sides to a larger center stone, creating a tapered effect. A tennis necklace uses uniform stones throughout. Both are continuous-set designs, but they read differently at the neck — the riviera draws attention to the center, the tennis necklace distributes light evenly across the full run.

**How should I store a diamond tennis necklace?**
Lay it flat or loosely coiled in a lined box. Never fold it sharply or pile it with other pieces. The hinges are precision-machined; repeated stress in one direction causes fatigue. A dedicated compartment or soft pouch keeps the links aligned and prevents the clasp from scratching adjacent jewelry.

**Does a tennis necklace need regular professional maintenance?**
Yes. Have the piece inspected once a year. The inspection covers prong tips, hinge tension, and clasp function. Prong tips wear at the ends from normal contact with clothing; catching them before a tip breaks prevents stone loss. This is a thirty-minute job for any competent bench jeweler and the cost is minimal relative to what it protects.
