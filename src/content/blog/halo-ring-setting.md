There is a moment, when you hold a well-made halo ring under light, where the center stone and the diamonds surrounding it stop looking like separate things. The boundary dissolves. What you see instead is a single, undivided field of brilliance — a stone that seems larger, warmer, more alive than the number on its certificate suggests.

Most jewelers approximate this effect. Bez Ambar engineered it.

## The Logic of the Halo

The idea behind a halo setting has been with us since the Georgian era. Surround a center stone with smaller diamonds and the eye, unable to locate the edge of the stone, perceives the whole composition as one large gem. It is a trick of optics, but an honest one — the light is real, the brilliance is real, and the effect on the finger is undeniable.

What has always compromised the halo is the gap. In a conventional pavé halo, small round diamonds are mounted in tiny individual prong settings. Between each stone, a sliver of metal is visible. Under light, these slivers interrupt the halo's continuity. The eye catches the seams. The illusion breaks, even slightly, at the moment it should be most persuasive.

For decades this was simply accepted as a technical limitation.

## A Different Kind of Stone

Bez Ambar's solution was not to refine the existing approach. It was to abandon it.

The Blaze® accent stone is square, not round. Each one is individually cut to match the geometry of its neighbor and the curvature of the center stone it surrounds. When set, these stones sit edge to edge, with no space between them — no prong gaps, no interruption, no seam.

The halo closes.

What results is a continuous perimeter of light that merges with the center stone rather than framing it from a distance. A 1.00-carat diamond in a Blaze® halo reads closer to 1.40 carats. The difference between those two stones in equivalent quality represents a significant investment. The Blaze® closes that gap for a fraction of the cost — not by hiding the stone's size, but by completing it.

Bez Ambar received a design patent for the Blaze® setting. It is available only here, or through an authorized Bez Ambar retailer.

## What the Halo Can Become

The halo is not a single thing. It is a structural idea that takes different shapes depending on what the piece is asking for.

A single halo is the essential form — one row of accent stones, clean and enduring, suited to every center shape. A double halo adds a second concentric ring, more elaborate in its intention, more dramatic in its presence. A hidden halo tucks accent diamonds beneath the center stone's basket, invisible from above, visible only as an unexpected source of warmth when the ring catches the light at an angle.

Then there are colored halos — sapphires, rubies, or fancy colored diamonds replacing white stones as the accent. The effect is one of contrast and character: the colored halo doesn't compete with the center stone, it converses with it.

The center stone shape matters less than people expect. Round brilliants and cushion cuts integrate most naturally with the halo's geometry. Ovals gain visual length. Princess cuts pair with a square halo frame that mirrors their own corners. The stone does not need to be exceptional to carry a halo well. That, in a certain sense, is the point.
