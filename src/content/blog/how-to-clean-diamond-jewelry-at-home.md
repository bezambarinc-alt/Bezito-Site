*A diamond is the hardest natural material on earth, but the film that accumulates on its surface from daily wear — body oil, lotion, soap residue, and cooking grease — does not need to be hard to make the stone look dead.*

---

### The Problem
## Why Diamond Jewelry Loses Brilliance Between Cleanings

A diamond appears brilliant because light enters through the table and crown facets, reflects off the pavilion facets, and exits back through the crown. This optical performance depends on clean, unobstructed facet surfaces. A thin film of skin oil on the diamond's table facet does not scratch the stone — diamond's hardness is too high for any common household substance to scratch it — but it does change the refractive behavior of light at the surface. Oil has a refractive index closer to glass than to air, and when a diamond's facet surface is coated with oil, the light behavior that produces fire and scintillation is substantially degraded.

Body chemistry is the primary source of contamination. Your hands produce natural oils, and every time you touch your ring the stone contacts that oil. Lotion, hand cream, sunscreen, and moisturizer accelerate the contamination cycle. Soap itself, counterintuitively, leaves a film — bar soap in particular deposits a waxy residue that adheres to prong settings and lodge beneath stones where it is impossible to remove without a brush. Cooking grease and cleaning products create the most tenacious films, which is why jewelry worn in the kitchen accumulates visible residue faster than jewelry worn in other environments.

The visual test for a contaminated stone is direct comparison in bright light. Hold the piece under a single direct light source and look at the stone from above. A clean diamond returns a bright, crisp reflection of the light source from each major facet. A contaminated diamond shows a dull, diffuse surface with scattered reflections that do not resolve into clear facet patterns. The difference is dramatic, and a client who has never cleaned their ring under this test is often surprised at how much brilliance has been lost.

### The Method
## How to Clean Diamond Jewelry Safely at Home

The correct home cleaning method for diamond jewelry is warm water, a small amount of mild dish soap, a soft toothbrush, and adequate rinsing. This is not a simplified version of a more complex professional process — it is the actual process, and it works.

Fill a small bowl with warm water — not hot, because thermal shock can stress certain stone settings, and not cold because warm water cuts oil more effectively. Add one or two drops of mild dish soap, the type with no harsh chemical additives or bleaching agents. Place the piece in the soapy water and allow it to soak for three to five minutes. The soak softens the contamination layer.

Remove the piece and use a soft-bristled toothbrush to gently scrub the stone and setting. Pay particular attention to the areas behind and beneath the stone where the most contamination accumulates, especially in prong settings where the skin-contact areas are most active. Use short, light strokes — the point is to dislodge loosened contamination, not to scrub mechanically. Rinse thoroughly under running warm water, covering the drain with a cloth or stopper in case the piece slips. Dry with a clean lint-free cloth or allow to air dry on a soft surface.

### What Not to Do
## Common Cleaning Mistakes That Damage Fine Jewelry

Chlorine bleach is the most dangerous household cleaner for gold jewelry. Chlorine attacks gold alloys at the grain boundaries of the metal, weakening the structure over time in a process called stress corrosion cracking. The damage is invisible at first and catastrophic when it manifests — a prong structure weakened by chlorine exposure can fracture suddenly under normal wear stress. Remove all gold jewelry before entering a chlorinated pool or hot tub, and never use bleach or bleach-containing cleaners on gold pieces.

Ultrasonic cleaners are available for home use and are effective, but they are not appropriate for all pieces. Ultrasonic cavitation can loosen pavé stones that are not perfectly seated, and it can aggravate existing cracks in included or surface-reaching natural stones. A home ultrasonic cleaner is safe for well-set, clean diamond jewelry with no known structural issues. It is not safe for estate pieces of unknown condition, pieces with large surface-reaching inclusions, or pieces with loose stones. When in doubt, use the warm-water-and-brush method instead.

Toothpaste is a popular home remedy for jewelry cleaning and a bad one. Most toothpastes contain mild abrasives that can scratch softer stones and gold settings. The abrasive level that is too mild to scratch a diamond is sufficient to scratch a gold surface to a matte finish. Avoid toothpaste for any metal-and-stone jewelry.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How often should I clean my diamond ring at home?**
For a ring worn daily, once every two weeks maintains the diamond at near-optimal brilliance. Clients who cook frequently, apply lotion regularly, or work with their hands should clean weekly. The cleaning takes ten minutes and the visual improvement is immediate. Do not wait until the diamond looks obviously cloudy — a stone cleaned regularly never reaches the worst contamination state.

**Can I use hand sanitizer to clean my jewelry?**
Hand sanitizer — the alcohol-gel type that became ubiquitous after 2020 — is not a cleaning solution for jewelry. The gel polymer in many formulations leaves a residue on the stone and setting that is as problematic as the original contamination. Alcohol-based sanitizer in liquid form will clean the stone surface but has no detergent action to remove oils from beneath stones and inside settings. Use proper warm water and dish soap.

**Is it safe to clean diamond earrings the same way as rings?**
Yes. The warm-water-and-brush method is safe for diamond studs and drop earrings. Remove the earring backs before cleaning so the post and backing are fully exposed. Pay attention to the area where the stone sits in its setting, which accumulates skin oil from contact with the earlobe. Rinse carefully and confirm the post and backing are fully dry before reattachment.

**Will cleaning affect the rhodium plating on white gold?**
The warm-water-and-dish-soap method does not remove rhodium plating. Rhodium plating is a durable electroplated surface that withstands routine cleaning. Rhodium plating is degraded over time by normal wear — contact with skin, friction against surfaces, and the mechanical action of removing the piece — not by gentle cleaning. Clean your white gold diamond jewelry without concern for the plating.
