*Rubies have decorated the hands of royalty and warlords for three thousand years — here is what that history tells you about buying one today.*

---

The ancient Burmese believed rubies were drops of blood from the earth's heart. The Sanskrit word for ruby — ratnaraj — translates as "king of precious stones." That reputation was not accidental. For most of recorded history, a fine ruby was rarer and more valuable than a diamond of comparable size, and in some periods, a good Burmese pigeon-blood ruby commanded prices that no diamond could touch.

Today that relationship has shifted, partly because diamond marketing reshaped public perception in the twentieth century, and partly because ruby mining has expanded well beyond Burma. But the underlying reality remains: a genuinely fine, unheated natural ruby from a premium origin is among the rarest and most valuable gemstones in the world. What has changed is that the category now spans an enormous range, from exceptional museum-quality stones to heavily treated material that is technically ruby but barely resembles the historic ideal.

Understanding where your stone falls on that range is the most important thing you can do before buying.

### Where Rubies
## Come From and Why It Matters

Burma — now Myanmar — specifically the Mogok Valley, is still the most prestigious ruby origin in the world. Mogok stones at their finest show what the trade calls pigeon-blood color: a saturated, slightly bluish red with a natural fluorescence that makes the stone appear to glow from within, especially in incandescent light. Burma origin adds a meaningful premium to a stone's price, but origin alone means nothing without quality. A pale, included Mogok stone is still a pale, included stone.

Mozambique entered the market seriously around 2009 and now produces rubies that rival Burma in color saturation. Many trade professionals consider the finest Mozambique stones competitive with Mogok material. Thailand produces darker, more iron-rich rubies often used in commercial jewelry. Madagascar, Vietnam, and Tanzania round out the major sources, each with its own color signature.

Laboratory reports from GIA or Gübelin that include origin determination matter here. An "origin: Burma" report from a reputable laboratory is a verifiable fact, not a seller's claim.

### Heat Treatment
## The Industry Standard You Must Understand

The overwhelming majority of rubies on the market — estimates run above ninety-five percent — have been heat treated. This is not a scandal; it is an industry norm. Heat treatment at temperatures between 1600 and 1900 degrees Celsius dissolves silk inclusions, improves clarity, and intensifies or corrects color. The result is a more beautiful stone, and the treatment is considered stable and permanent.

What is not standard, and what requires careful disclosure, is fracture filling. Some rubies have their surface-reaching fractures filled with lead glass or other substances to dramatically improve apparent clarity. A stone that looks eye-clean in the showcase may be forty percent glass by volume. Fracture-filled rubies are dramatically cheaper because they are dramatically less durable — the filling can be damaged by ultrasonic cleaning, common household chemicals, or even the heat of a jeweler's torch during repair.

Ask specifically: is this stone fracture-filled? Require a laboratory report. GIA will note "indications of lead glass filling" if it is present.

### Ruby's Place
## on the Hardness Scale

Diamond sits at 10 on the Mohs hardness scale. Ruby and sapphire — both corundum — sit at 9. That single-step difference is larger than it sounds because the Mohs scale is not linear: diamond is roughly four times harder than corundum, not ten percent harder. Nevertheless, ruby at 9 is genuinely hard, genuinely suitable for daily wear, and far more resistant to scratching than any other colored stone except sapphire.

The main durability concern with ruby is not hardness but toughness. Some rubies contain significant internal fractures, and a hard blow at the right angle can cause a stone to chip or cleave along those planes. This is why clarity matters beyond aesthetics: a heavily included ruby is a more fragile ruby. In a ring that will be worn every day, this consideration is real.

A well-chosen ruby in a protective setting — bezel, half-bezel, or a prong arrangement that keeps the girdle away from impact — is a practical choice for an engagement ring. I have made many over forty years, and clients who chose them with care wear them without incident.

### Price, Size,
## and Setting Strategy

Fine, unheated, Burmese pigeon-blood rubies above three carats are among the most expensive gemstones at auction. For engagement ring purposes, most clients work with heat-treated stones, which are beautiful and appropriate, and the price difference versus unheated material is significant enough to allow a substantially larger or better-quality stone.

Color is the dominant value driver. Saturation matters more than size: a vivid, well-saturated one-carat ruby is a more impressive stone than a pale, washed-out two-carat. Secondary hue is important — purplish-red reads richer than orangey-red to most eyes, and the trade premiums reflect this. Avoid stones that appear too dark, trending toward brownish or inky; they lose brilliance in low light.

Yellow gold is the traditional pairing for ruby because the warm metal amplifies the stone's warmth. Rose gold does the same with a slightly more contemporary result. White gold and platinum create a starker, more graphic contrast — not wrong, but a different aesthetic choice. The setting should protect the stone's edges while keeping the table open to light.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is a ruby engagement ring a practical choice for everyday wear?**

Ruby ranks 9 on the Mohs hardness scale, which makes it the second hardest gemstone after diamond and genuinely resistant to daily abrasion. The more relevant durability question is the stone's clarity: a heavily fractured ruby is more vulnerable to chipping from impact than a cleaner stone. Choose a ruby with decent clarity, set it in a design that protects the girdle, and it will wear well for decades.

**What does "pigeon-blood" actually mean when describing a ruby?**

Pigeon-blood is a trade term for the finest Burmese rubies, referring to a specific quality of pure red with a slight blue secondary hue and a characteristic fluorescence that makes the stone appear luminous in all lighting conditions. It is not a formal grading category — it is a market description — but laboratories like GIA and Gübelin do use the term in their reports when a stone qualifies, and it carries a meaningful price premium.

**Should I pay more for an unheated ruby?**

If you are buying a ruby as an investment or a collector's piece and the stone is truly exceptional, the unheated premium is justified because unheated fine rubies are genuinely rare. For a beautiful engagement ring, a heat-treated ruby from a reputable source with a clean laboratory report is a perfectly appropriate choice, and the price difference allows you to buy a significantly better or larger stone within the same budget.

**How do I tell if a ruby has been fracture-filled?**

You need a laboratory report from GIA, Gübelin, or a similarly reputable gemological laboratory. Fracture filling is visible under magnification and will be noted on any credible report. Do not rely on a jeweler's verbal assurance or a seller's declaration — require the certificate. If a seller resists providing one, that is your answer.

**What carat size makes sense for a ruby engagement ring?**

Unlike diamonds, ruby does not have a cultural benchmark carat weight. Color and quality matter more than size in ruby, and a vivid, well-saturated half-carat ruby will outperform a pale one-carat stone in visual impact and in long-term value. That said, for an engagement ring centerpiece, stones in the one-to-two carat range strike the balance most clients find satisfying — present enough to read clearly on the hand without requiring a budget reserved for investment-grade rarities.
