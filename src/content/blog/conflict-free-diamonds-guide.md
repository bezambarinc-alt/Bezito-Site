*The Kimberley Process does one specific thing: it certifies that rough diamonds did not finance rebel movements opposing recognized governments. It does not certify working conditions, environmental practices, or political violence committed by those same governments.*

---

### What the Kimberley Process Is
## The Origin and Scope of the Certification System

The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme (KPCS) was established in 2003 following the international focus on "blood diamonds" — rough diamonds used to fund armed rebel groups, most prominently the RUF (Revolutionary United Front) in Sierra Leone and UNITA in Angola during the civil conflicts of the 1990s. The scheme is a joint agreement between 55 participating countries, the diamond industry, and civil society organizations. It requires that rough diamond shipments between participating countries be accompanied by a certificate verifying that the stones did not originate from rebel-controlled areas.

Under the Kimberley Process, member nations agree not to trade in uncertified rough diamonds and to implement internal controls that prevent non-compliant stones from entering their supply chains. Each shipment receives a tamper-resistant certificate with a unique serial number. The system covers rough diamonds only — polished stones are not individually certified under the KPCS, though member nations' compliance at the rough stage is intended to ensure that the polished inventory from legitimate sources is traceable.

The KPCS has achieved its stated goal in specific cases: Sierra Leone and Angola are no longer significant sources of conflict diamonds, and the rebel funding mechanism that prompted the scheme's creation has been disrupted in those specific geographies. The system represents a genuine international coordination achievement for a specific, defined problem.

### What the Kimberley Process Does Not Cover
## The Documented Gaps in the Certification

The Kimberley Process defines "conflict diamond" narrowly as a stone that funds "rebel movements or their allies seeking to undermine legitimate governments." This definition excludes several categories of concern that human rights organizations and buyers commonly associate with ethical sourcing. Violence perpetrated by recognized governments — including the governments of several major diamond-producing nations — falls outside the KPCS definition. Labor abuses, including dangerous working conditions, child labor, and exploitation of artisanal miners, are not addressed. Environmental damage from mining operations is outside the scheme's scope.

Zimbabwe's Marange diamond fields became the most publicized example of this gap. When government forces seized the Marange fields in 2008, documented reports from NGOs (non-governmental organizations) and journalists described killings, torture, and forced labor by Zimbabwean security forces. A 2009 decision by Kimberley Process member states allowed Zimbabwean diamonds from Marange to be certified as conflict-free because the violence was committed by the recognized government rather than a rebel movement. The KPCS definition was satisfied; the ethical concerns were not.

Civil society members of the KPCS, including Global Witness — one of the founding organizations — have resigned from the process at various points, citing the scheme's inability to address its documented gaps. The KPCS remains the operational standard because no comprehensive alternative has been established, but buyers who understand its scope know that a Kimberley Process certificate is an answer to one specific question, not a comprehensive ethical assurance.

### What Stronger Assurances Look Like
## Beyond Kimberley Process: Chain of Custody and Transparency

Some mining companies and retailers have implemented chain-of-custody tracking that goes beyond the KPCS minimum. Botswana, through Debswana (a joint venture between the Botswana government and De Beers), offers one of the strongest traceability programs in the industry, with social investment programs tied to diamond revenues and documented working conditions. Canadian diamonds, produced under strict federal environmental regulations with significant First Nations consultation processes, carry strong third-party documentation for several concerns beyond the KPCS scope.

Individual retailers who have committed to specific origin traceability beyond KPCS certification — providing mine-of-origin documentation for specific stones — offer meaningfully stronger assurance than KPCS certification alone. These programs exist but are less prevalent than the industry's general marketing around "conflict-free" would suggest, which often conflates KPCS compliance with a broader ethical standard that the KPCS does not actually represent.

Lab-grown diamonds offer a different form of ethical assurance. They bypass the mining supply chain entirely, eliminating all concerns about mining conditions, geographic origin, and government-violence exclusions from the KPCS definition. The trade-off is that the economic contribution to mining communities in producing countries is also bypassed — a choice with its own social consequences. Neither choice is free of ethical considerations; they simply carry different ones.

### Making a Buying Decision
## What Matters and What to Ask

Buyers who want stronger ethical assurance than the KPCS baseline should ask their jeweler specific questions rather than accepting "conflict-free" as a sufficient answer. The useful questions are: Can you provide the country of origin for this rough diamond? Is there mine-of-origin documentation beyond the Kimberley Process certificate? What percentage of your inventory comes from producers with labor practice documentation?

A dealer who can answer these questions with specifics is operating at a meaningfully higher transparency standard than the industry minimum. A dealer who responds with "all our diamonds are Kimberley Process certified" has answered a narrower question than the one you asked. Both answers tell you something about the dealer's supply chain practices.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does "conflict-free" mean the diamond was ethically sourced?**
Not comprehensively. A conflict-free certificate under the Kimberley Process means the diamond did not finance rebel movements opposing recognized governments. It does not address labor conditions, environmental practices, or violence by governments themselves. If broader ethical assurance is important to you, ask for mine-of-origin documentation or consider lab-grown diamonds.

**Are Canadian diamonds really conflict-free?**
Canadian diamonds are produced under federal environmental regulations, significant labor protections, and First Nations consultation frameworks that are among the strictest in the world for mining operations. They carry strong third-party documentation for several ethical dimensions that KPCS does not address. Canada is not a conflict zone, and the political conditions that generate conflict diamonds do not apply. Canadian origin is among the most reliably documented in the natural diamond market.

**Is a Kimberley Process certificate the same as a GIA certificate?**
No. They are completely different documents. A Kimberley Process certificate accompanies rough diamond shipments at the country level and certifies conflict-free origin under the KPCS definition. A GIA certificate is a laboratory grading report for a specific polished stone, documenting its color, clarity, cut, and carat weight. Both can exist for the same stone; they certify different things.

**Do lab-grown diamonds avoid all ethical issues?**
Lab-grown diamonds avoid all mining-related ethical concerns, including the KPCS gaps. The ethical considerations that do apply to lab-grown diamonds relate to energy consumption in the manufacturing process (which depends on the energy source used by the producer) and the economic impact on diamond mining communities in producing countries. A buyer who shifts from natural to lab-grown eliminates one category of ethical concern while accepting a different one.

**What happened to conflict diamonds from the 1990s?**
The major rebel-funded conflicts that prompted the Kimberley Process — in Sierra Leone and Angola — ended in the early 2000s, and the specific diamond-for-arms pipelines that characterized those conflicts have been disrupted in those geographies. Conflict diamonds as defined by the KPCS represent a far smaller share of the global supply now than in the late 1990s. The ongoing concern is not a return to 1990s-scale conflict diamond flows, but the narrowness of the KPCS definition in addressing current human rights concerns in the diamond supply chain.
