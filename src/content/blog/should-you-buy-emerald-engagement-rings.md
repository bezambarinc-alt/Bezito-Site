*Emeralds are the greenest thing in fine jewelry, and they are also the most requiring. Understanding what they are and how they behave is the honest basis for the decision.*

---

No other gemstone produces the green of a fine emerald. The deep, saturated green of a top Colombian stone is a color that no other material replicates, not tsavorite garnet, not green sapphire, not chrome tourmaline. If you want that specific green, there is only one stone.

The question of whether an emerald belongs in an engagement ring is worth answering honestly, because there are real considerations alongside the genuine appeal.

### What an Emerald
## Actually Is

Emerald is the green variety of beryl, a mineral family that also includes aquamarine (blue-green), morganite (pink), and heliodor (yellow). The green in emerald comes from chromium and occasionally vanadium impurities in the crystal structure.

The primary sources are Colombia (historically the most prestigious, producing the deepest and most saturated green), Zambia (produces a bluish-green with high transparency that has become widely respected), and Brazil (largest volume, wide quality range). Colombian emeralds from the Muzo and Chivor mines in particular command significant premiums.

Emeralds are softer than diamonds and sapphires. Their hardness is 7.5 to 8 on the Mohs scale, which means they can be scratched by quartz (7), a mineral found in ordinary dust and sand. This is not hypothetical: emeralds in rings worn daily accumulate surface scratches more readily than sapphires or diamonds.

### The Jardin
## Inclusions

Almost all natural emeralds contain inclusions, fractures, and internal features. The French term jardin, meaning garden, describes the internal world of inclusions within an emerald. Unlike diamonds, where inclusions are graded primarily as quality flaws, the jardin of an emerald is accepted as a characteristic of the stone rather than a defect.

This means that emeralds are graded differently from diamonds. An emerald that would receive an I1 clarity grade if it were a diamond is not a flawed emerald; it is a normal emerald. Fine Colombian emeralds often have significant internal characteristics that are completely accepted at their price point.

Eye-clean emeralds, where no inclusions are visible to the naked eye, do exist but are rare and command a substantial premium. For most buyers, the question is not whether the emerald has inclusions but whether the specific inclusions in a specific stone are acceptable to you when you view it in person.

### Treatment
## is Standard

Cedar oil, resin, and epoxy treatments to fill surface-reaching fractures and improve the apparent clarity and color of emeralds are industry standard. GIA uses a scale from None to Significant to describe the extent of clarity enhancement. "Faint" enhancement is very common and accepted. "Significant" enhancement, where a large proportion of the stone's apparent clarity comes from filler rather than the emerald itself, is worth knowing about before purchasing.

Cedar oil treatment is the most traditional and accepted. Resin treatments that introduce artificial color are less accepted and should be avoided. A GIA or AGL report on a significant emerald purchase will specify the type and extent of enhancement.

### The Care
## Requirement

Ultrasonic cleaners can damage emeralds by driving cleaning solution into existing fractures and expanding them. Steam cleaners can dissolve the oil or resin that fills fractures, degrading the stone's clarity. Harsh chemicals, including common household cleaners, affect the oils used in treatment.

Clean emerald jewelry in warm soapy water with a soft brush. Have the enhancement refreshed periodically by a jeweler experienced with colored stones. Avoid exposing the ring to sharp impacts, particularly on the stone itself, because emeralds can cleave along internal fracture planes.

These are real requirements. For an engagement ring worn every day by someone who is not prepared to maintain it this way, an emerald may be the wrong choice. For someone who understands what the stone needs and is committed to that care, an emerald engagement ring is a spectacular and distinct piece that will remain so.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Are emeralds too fragile for daily wear in an engagement ring?**
Fragile is the wrong word. Emeralds are softer than diamonds and sapphires and require more care. They can be chipped by sharp impacts and scratched by harder materials. A protective setting, such as a bezel or a setting with low prongs that do not project the stone, reduces risk. Many people wear emerald rings daily for decades without problems; it requires awareness and appropriate care.

**What does jardin mean in an emerald?**
Jardin is French for garden, and it describes the internal inclusions and fractures within an emerald. In emeralds, these internal features are accepted as characteristic rather than treated as defects. Most natural emeralds have visible jardin. An eye-clean emerald with minimal internal features is rare and commands a significant premium.

**Is emerald treatment a problem?**
Standard emerald treatment with cedar oil or resin is accepted throughout the trade and disclosed on laboratory reports. It is not a problem if the extent is disclosed and appropriate. Significant resin treatment that masks poor quality is worth avoiding. A GIA or AGL report will specify the type and extent of any enhancement.

**How do I clean an emerald ring?**
In warm soapy water with a soft brush. Do not use ultrasonic cleaners, steam, or harsh chemicals. These can damage the stone's clarity enhancement. Take the ring to a jeweler annually for inspection and periodic refreshing of the oil treatment if needed.

**How does the price of an emerald compare to a diamond?**
Fine Colombian emeralds of significant size and saturated color can be more expensive per carat than comparable white diamonds. At the commercial quality level, emeralds are less expensive than equivalent diamonds. Price depends heavily on origin, color saturation, transparency, and extent of treatment.
