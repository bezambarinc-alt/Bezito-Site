*The trillion cut is not a minor variation on familiar shapes — it is a fundamentally different geometric approach to the diamond, with visual and structural consequences that buyers need to understand before choosing it.*

### The Shape's Identity
## What a Trillion Cut Is and Where It Comes From

The trillion cut — sometimes called trilliant, triangular brilliant, or triangle cut — is a diamond fashioned into a triangular outline with three equal sides and brilliant-style faceting that maximizes light return within the triangular geometry. The cut was developed in Amsterdam in the 1970s, with the name "Trilliant" originally trademarked by the Henry Meyer Diamond Company. The more generic "trillion" has since entered the trade lexicon and refers to any triangular brilliant cut without specific attribution.

The trillion's triangular outline is its most immediately striking visual quality. It reads as a bold, architectural shape with no precedent in the major historical cutting styles — no antique trillion exists the way antique cushions and old European cuts do, because the precision required to cut a triangular brilliant cleanly was not achievable with pre-modern cutting tools. The shape is entirely a product of twentieth-century technology.

Trillion cuts occur in two main configurations. A curved-side trillion has slightly bowed edges rather than perfectly straight sides, which distributes mass slightly differently and softens the angular reading. A straight-side trillion has precise, flat edges that create a true equilateral triangle. Curved-side trillions are more common in contemporary cutting; straight-side trillions are associated with some older production and with very specific design applications where geometric precision is the goal.

### Optical Behavior
## How Trillions Handle Light Differently From Other Shapes

The triangular geometry creates optical patterns distinct from round or rectangular brilliants. Light entering a trillion distributes across three main facet groups that mirror the three-fold symmetry of the shape. The result is a distinctive, pinwheel-like scintillation pattern — light flashes rotate around the center of the stone in a pattern that does not occur in four-fold symmetric shapes. This optical signature is immediately recognizable to anyone familiar with the cut and is one of its primary aesthetic distinctions.

Trillions also exhibit more fire — spectral color dispersion — than rounds of equivalent carat weight in many cases, because the steep crown angles used in trillion cutting to maximize the triangular outline's light return produce strong dispersive refraction. The fire-to-brightness ratio is different from round brilliants: trillions can appear more colorful and less purely white, which is a desirable quality for clients seeking a warmer, more dramatic optical performance.

The triangular outline's three points are the same vulnerability in trillions that corners are in princess cuts. The points concentrate mechanical stress in the diamond's most acute geometric features, and impacts at the point tips can chip the stone. Good trillion settings use V-tip prongs at all three corners, identical in function to the corner prongs used for princess cuts. Settings that leave the points exposed are invitations to damage.

### Primary Uses
## Center Stone and Side Stone Applications

As a center stone, the trillion creates a bold, distinctive ring that reads as unconventional without being eccentric. It works best in minimalist settings that let the shape speak without competing decorative elements. A simple three-prong solitaire — one prong per corner — in platinum is the cleanest presentation. More elaborate settings risk cluttering the clean geometric reading of the triangular outline.

The trillion's most commercially significant use is as a side stone in three-stone rings flanking a larger center diamond. In this configuration, two trillion-cut diamonds of matched weight and quality are set on either side of the center stone, with the trillion's flat edge facing the center. The result is a composition where the geometric contrast between the triangular flanks and the center stone (typically a round, oval, or cushion) creates visual tension and movement. This is among the most enduring three-stone ring designs in the contemporary market, and it uses the trillion's shape advantages without asking the shape to carry the ring's entire visual identity.

As accent stones in more complex compositions — in channel borders, as halo elements, or in the gallery of a more elaborate setting — the trillion's triangular outline creates geometric complexity that most jewelers use sparingly. It is not a filling shape the way round brilliants or baguettes are; its distinctive geometry asserts itself wherever it appears.

### Sizing and Pricing
## What Trillion Cuts Cost and What Sizes to Choose

Trillion cuts in the 0.25 to 0.75 carat range are the most common sizes because they are most frequently used as matched pairs for three-stone settings. Matched pairs require both stones to be very closely matched in size, color, and cut quality — a visually obvious mismatch between two side stones is one of the most common complaints in three-stone ring service. When buying trillion side stones, always specify matched pairs from the same cutting lot and verify the match visually before purchase.

Trillion center stones above one carat are available but less common, because the triangular shape does not cut as efficiently from round diamond rough as oval or cushion shapes. The rough material required to produce a clean triangular brilliant at one carat or above is relatively wasteful in terms of carat recovery, which pushes per-carat prices for large trillions higher than equivalent-weight ovals or radiant cuts.

Color and clarity grading for trillions follows the same GIA standards as other fancy shapes. VS2 or better for eye-clean clarity; G or above for white metal settings. The triangular outline shows body color comparably to oval and cushion cuts — more readily than round brilliants, less readily than step cuts.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is a trillion cut durable enough for an engagement ring?**
The trillion's three points are its vulnerability — they must be protected by properly placed V-tip prongs at each corner. With correct setting and reasonable daily care, a trillion center stone is a wearable choice. The shape is harder to source in very large sizes and is more specialized in its setting requirements than rounds or ovals, but these are not reasons to avoid it if the aesthetic is what you want.

**Can trillion cuts be used as center stones?**
Yes, and with the right setting — typically a simple three-prong solitaire in platinum or white gold — a trillion center stone creates a dramatically distinctive ring. The shape is unconventional enough that it reads as a deliberate, knowledgeable aesthetic choice rather than a default selection. For clients who specifically want a non-round, non-oval ring with strong visual identity, the trillion center stone delivers that clearly.

**What is the best length-to-width ratio for a trillion cut?**
An equilateral trillion — equal sides — is the standard and most aesthetically balanced form. Some fancy trillions have slightly elongated or asymmetric proportions; these are specialty cuts for specific design applications. For side-stone pairs, perfect equilateral proportion matching between the two stones is essential. For center stones, the equilateral form reads most cleanly in most settings.

**Are trillion diamonds cheaper than round brilliants?**
Per carat, trillions are typically less expensive than round brilliants of equivalent quality because they do not have the same demand premium. As side stones in matched pairs, they are often priced as a pair rather than individually. For center stones above one carat, the limited supply can push per-carat prices higher than comparable oval or cushion shapes in some market conditions.

**What metals work best with trillion cut diamonds?**
Platinum and white gold are the most neutral choices and let the diamond's triangular outline read most clearly against the setting. Yellow gold can be used intentionally for warmth, particularly with lower color grades. The V-tip prongs protecting the corners are equally effective in any metal. The metal choice should follow the wearer's preference and the overall design language rather than being dictated by the shape.
