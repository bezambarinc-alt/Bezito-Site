### The Structure
## Why the Tennis Bracelet Is the Most Demanding Piece to Make Well

A tennis bracelet is a continuous line of individually set diamonds running the full circumference of the wrist. Every link hinges to the next, every stone sits in its own setting, and the whole assembly must flex in multiple directions without any link stressing the setting beside it. At seven inches — the average wrist circumference for women — a bracelet carrying 0.10-carat stones may contain seventy or more individual diamonds. At 0.25 carats per stone, the count drops to twenty-eight. Every variation in that combination changes the visual character, the price, and the fabrication demands.

The number that controls everything else is the ratio of stone diameter to link width. If the stone is close in diameter to the link width, the bracelet reads as a continuous diamond surface with minimal visible metal. If the stone diameter is significantly smaller than the link width, you see gold first and diamonds second. Most quality tennis bracelets are engineered so that the stone-to-metal ratio from the face shows approximately 60 to 70 percent diamond, 30 to 40 percent metal. This ratio is a fabrication decision, not an accident.

Setting style determines both visual character and long-term maintenance demands. Four-prong settings hold each stone at four corners with the sides open — maximum light enters the stone from the sides, and brilliance is at its highest. The tradeoff is that four prong tips per stone, multiplied by seventy-plus stones, creates hundreds of individual tip points that must be inspected annually. Bezel settings wrap each stone in a continuous metal wall — no exposed tips, lower maintenance, slightly reduced side light entry. Channel settings place stones in a continuous groove between two metal rails — the stones appear to float in a metal channel and the visual line is very clean, but the channel must be precisely calibrated so no stone rocks in the setting.

### The Diamonds
## Stone Matching Across the Full Bracelet

The matching standards for a tennis bracelet are more demanding than for any other diamond piece, because a bracelet worn on the wrist is examined from inches away in direct light by everyone around the table at dinner. Every stone in the full run must be calibrated to the same diameter within 0.1mm. Color must grade consistently — one warm stone in a row of cool stones will be immediately visible. Clarity should be uniform enough that no single stone appears darker or more included than its neighbors.

We sort tennis bracelet stones by diameter and color before any other grading is applied, because the eye catches inconsistency in these two variables first. A bracelet where every stone grades G color individually but where three stones read slightly warmer than the others is a bracelet that does not pass our selection standard. The laboratory grade of each individual stone matters less than how the stones look together in the assembled piece under consistent lighting.

Total carat weight is the number most often advertised for tennis bracelets, but it is the least useful single metric for comparing quality across pieces. Three carats spread across seventy stones in a four-prong setting in SI1 clarity diamonds is a very different piece from three carats spread across twenty-eight stones in a bezel setting in VS1 clarity. Both state "three carats." The price difference between them will be substantial, and the visual character differs as well. Specify stone count, per-stone size, clarity, and setting type when comparing pieces across vendors.

### The Clasp
## The One Component That Determines Whether You Keep the Bracelet Forever

The clasp on a tennis bracelet is not a decorative detail — it is the most structurally critical component of the piece. A tennis bracelet worn daily experiences thousands of openings and closings per year. The clasp must hold under the force of an accidental snag, the tension of a tight fit, and the repeated mechanical stress of opening and closing. Clasp failure on a tennis bracelet means losing the entire piece in a single moment.

Quality tennis bracelets use a box clasp with at least one double-locking mechanism — a tab that folds into the box and is then secured by a secondary catch. The double lock requires two deliberate actions to open, which prevents accidental opening. Some better bracelets add a figure-eight safety as a tertiary retention device on the hinge side of the clasp, which catches the bracelet even if both primary locks fail simultaneously.

The clasp should be integrated into the bracelet's visual line rather than appearing as a structural interruption. On a well-designed tennis bracelet, the clasp link is set with the same diamonds as the adjacent links and is detectable only by a slight difference in the link's hinge mechanism. If you can identify the clasp visually from across the table, the bracelet's design is compromised.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What total carat weight is right for everyday wear?**
For a bracelet worn regularly, the per-stone size matters more than total carat weight. Stones in the 0.10 to 0.15-carat range per stone produce a bracelet that sits comfortably, does not feel heavy on the wrist, and delivers clear diamond presence. Stones above 0.25 carats per stone produce a more substantial visual statement but also more weight and more maintenance surface area. Two to three total carats in the 0.10 to 0.15-carat-per-stone range is the most practical everyday range.

**Should a tennis bracelet fit tightly or loosely?**
A tennis bracelet should fit with approximately a quarter inch of clearance between the bracelet and the wrist — enough that it lies flat against the skin without binding, but not so loose that it slides toward the hand when the wrist is angled. A bracelet that fits too tightly puts repeated stress on the link hinges from the restriction of natural wrist movement. A bracelet that fits too loosely slides and rotates, stressing the clasp asymmetrically.

**How often should a tennis bracelet be serviced?**
Annually, at minimum. Annual service includes prong tip inspection, hinge tension check, clasp function test, and light polishing if needed. On a bracelet with four-prong settings, the service should check each prong tip across all stones — a competent bench jeweler can complete this inspection in thirty to forty-five minutes. Missing one year of service on a regularly worn tennis bracelet is not catastrophic; missing three years creates real risk of a stone loss.

**Can the length of a tennis bracelet be adjusted?**
Yes. Adding links requires sourcing matched stones, which is straightforward if we made the original piece. Removing links is simpler and can be done at any competent bench. The clasp should remain centered on the bracelet after adjustment — length changes must be balanced so that equal numbers of links appear on each side of the clasp.

**Is platinum or white gold better for a tennis bracelet?**
Platinum is heavier and more durable than white gold and will not change color over time. White gold requires rhodium plating to maintain its white appearance, typically every two to three years depending on wear frequency. For a bracelet worn daily, platinum's maintenance-free white color is a genuine advantage. The additional cost of platinum over white gold is offset over a decade of ownership by the elimination of replating costs.
