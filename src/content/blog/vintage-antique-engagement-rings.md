*A ring made before your grandmother was born can outlast the next hundred years — if you know what to look for before you buy.*

### Era Matters
## The Period Defines Everything About the Stone

Antique and vintage are not interchangeable terms, and the difference carries real money. A ring qualifies as antique when it is at least 100 years old — that means Georgian (pre-1840s), Victorian (1837–1901), Edwardian (1901–1915), or early Art Deco (1920s). Vintage typically refers to pieces from the 1930s through the 1980s. Each era produced its own cutting technology, alloy preferences, and design vocabulary, and each of those variables affects value, wearability, and long-term durability.

Georgian pieces were almost always set in silver or gold over silver, because platinum was not yet workable. The stones are typically table-cut or rose-cut diamonds — flat on top, domed on the back — with none of the brilliance we associate with modern rounds. Victorian rings shifted toward yellow gold and began using mine-cut diamonds, which are taller and chunkier than a modern round but hold fire in candlelight in a way that is genuinely arresting. Edwardian work introduced platinum filigree so delicate it looks like lacework. Art Deco brought geometric precision, calibré-cut colored stones, and high-contrast black enamel.

Knowing the era tells you what to expect under magnification and what repairs are even possible. A Georgian closed-back setting cannot accept a GIA-graded stone without destroying the mounting. An Edwardian platinum filigree ring will not survive daily dish-washing. These are not defects — they are engineering facts about a different century.

### Condition and Cuts
## Old Cuts Are Not Old Problems

The single most misunderstood issue in antique ring buying is the old mine cut and the old European cut. Buyers trained on modern round brilliants often assume these cuts are inferior. They are not inferior — they are different. Old mine cuts have a higher crown, a smaller table, and a larger culet (the facet at the bottom tip of the stone). That large culet, visible as a circle when you look down through the table, is a feature of hand-polishing, not a flaw. Old European cuts, which appeared from roughly 1890 to 1930, are rounder in outline and have 58 facets like a modern round, but the proportions are entirely different.

What matters for condition is the girdle — the thin edge where the crown and pavilion meet. Old cuts were often polished to a very thin girdle, which chips. Examine the girdle under 10x magnification before purchasing any piece. Chips there are not always catastrophic, but they need to be factored into the price and may require recutting, which will reduce carat weight.

### Metal and Marks
## Hallmarks Tell You What the Jeweler Cannot

The metal in an antique ring is the one thing that cannot be faked without destroying the piece. British pieces carry assay office hallmarks — a date letter, a maker's mark, a purity symbol, and the assay office's own stamp. French pieces carry eagle heads or owl marks. American pieces from the Victorian and Edwardian periods were rarely hallmarked by law, but reputable makers stamped their work with karat values and house marks. Learn to read these marks or buy from a dealer who can read them for you and put that reading in writing.

Gold alloys in very old pieces behave differently than modern alloys. Victorian 15-karat gold — a standard that no longer exists — is harder and more yellow than 14-karat but softer than 18-karat. Platinum from the Edwardian period was mixed with iridium rather than ruthenium, which affects the way it polishes and the color of the surface over time.

### The Purchase
## Documentation and Independent Appraisal Are Not Optional

Buy any antique ring only with written documentation of the period attribution, the metal composition, and the stone identity. If the seller cannot provide those three things in writing, move on. Then take the ring to an independent appraiser — not affiliated with the seller — before the return window closes. A Graduate Gemologist (G.G.) from the Gemological Institute of America (GIA) is the standard credential. Ask specifically whether the diamond has been clarity-enhanced, fracture-filled, or laser-drilled. These treatments were applied to older stones as a commercial upgrade and are not always disclosed.

Insurance replacement value and resale value are two different numbers. An antique ring appraised at $12,000 for insurance purposes may sell for $6,000 on the secondary market. Know which number matters to you before you negotiate the purchase price.

Forty years of buying and selling antique pieces has taught me one rule above all others: the provenance story is not the ring. What the ring actually is — its metal, its stone, its condition — is what you are paying for. The story is a bonus. Verify the ring first. Enjoy the story after.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Can an old mine cut diamond be recut into a modern brilliant?**
Yes, and it happens regularly, but recutting permanently alters the stone. You will lose weight — sometimes as much as 15 to 20 percent — and you will lose the character that makes an old mine cut collectible. Recut only if the original cut has structural damage that cannot be polished out.

**How do I know if an antique ring has been repaired or altered?**
A qualified appraiser will look for solder seams, inconsistencies in metal color under UV light, and prongs that do not match the original fabrication style. Repairs are not automatically disqualifying, but undisclosed major alterations — adding shanks, replacing heads, converting from silver to gold — affect the integrity and value of the piece.

**Are antique engagement rings durable enough for everyday wear?**
It depends entirely on the era and the style. A heavy Victorian gold ring with a bezel-set stone is extremely durable. An Edwardian platinum filigree ring with claw-set stones and openwork panels is not designed for someone who works with their hands. A jeweler should assess the specific piece and tell you whether the prongs and mounting can handle daily wear without quarterly maintenance.

**What is the difference between an estate ring and an antique ring?**
Estate simply means previously owned — a ring sold at an estate sale could be five years old or five hundred years old. Antique means the piece is at least 100 years old, verified by period construction, hallmarks, and style. Do not let a seller use "estate" and "antique" interchangeably when you are paying antique prices.

**Is a GIA report available for antique diamonds?**
GIA grades loose stones regardless of age. If the diamond can be removed from the setting without damaging either the stone or the mounting, it can be submitted for a GIA Colored Diamond Grading Report or a Diamond Dossier. Many antique stones — particularly old mine cuts — have been submitted and graded. A GIA report on an antique diamond adds buyer confidence but does not change what the stone is. The grade is still the grade.
