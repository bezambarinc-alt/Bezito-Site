*Heart shaped diamonds are among the most technically demanding cuts in the gem trade — and buyers who know what to look for get far more for their money than those who don't.*

---

### The Cut
## Why Cutting a Heart Shaped Diamond Is Harder Than It Looks

A round brilliant has one job: return light straight back to the eye. A heart shaped diamond has to do that while also holding a recognizable silhouette — two symmetrical lobes, a precise cleft, and a pointed culet — all of which can collapse into something that looks more like a deflated balloon than a romantic gem if the cutter rushes the work.

The cleft, the indentation at the top center, is where most poorly cut hearts fail first. It needs to be sharp, clean, and centered. If it drifts even slightly off axis, the two lobes read as unequal, and the whole shape looks off. The wings — the outer curves of each lobe — must arc smoothly and meet the point at a clean, symmetrical angle. These are demands that require a skilled cutter working slowly, and that craftsmanship is reflected in the price of a well-executed stone.

Length-to-width ratio is the second variable buyers consistently underestimate. A ratio between 0.98 and 1.02 produces a heart that reads as balanced and proportional. Go much longer and the stone looks like a teardrop. Go too wide and it looks squat. When you are buying online or from a catalog image, always ask for the exact measurements — do not rely on what looks balanced in a tiny thumbnail.

### Optical Performance
## What to Look for in Light Return and Symmetry

A heart shaped diamond does not receive a cut grade from the Gemological Institute of America (GIA) the way a round brilliant does. GIA grades heart shapes on polish and symmetry only, which means the optical performance — how well the stone actually handles light — falls entirely to the buyer's eye and the seller's expertise.

The classic tool for evaluating light performance in fancy shapes is an ASET (Angular Spectrum Evaluation Tool) image. Red zones in an ASET image indicate light collected from the best angles; blue zones indicate light leakage. A well-cut heart will show rich, evenly distributed red across both lobes with minimal blue or white. If a seller cannot produce an ASET image on request, treat that as information.

Bow-tie shadows are common in elongated fancy shapes — ovals, pears, marquises — and hearts are not immune. A faint bow-tie is acceptable and nearly unavoidable. A pronounced dark bow-tie running across the center of the stone is a sign the crown angles were not correctly optimized, and no setting will fix it.

### Setting Choices
## How the Right Setting Protects and Showcases the Stone

The point of a heart shaped diamond is the most structurally vulnerable part of the stone. A hard knock to an unprotected culet can chip the tip cleanly, and a chip at the point of a heart is both visible and expensive to repair. A V-prong at the tip is not optional — it is a technical requirement. Any jeweler recommending a bezel or a simple claw prong at the point without explaining this tradeoff is not thinking carefully about the long-term life of the piece.

The lobes at the top benefit from prongs placed at the widest part of each curve. Three prongs total across the two lobes — one at each outer shoulder and one at the cleft — is a time-tested approach. Some designers use four, adding a prong at the inner edge of each lobe, for added security in high-wear settings.

Metal choice affects how the shape reads visually. White gold and platinum let the diamond's outline read clearly against the hand. Yellow gold, particularly 18-karat, warms the color of the stone and softens the silhouette — well suited to buyers who want a more romantic, vintage feel.

### Scale and Size
## How Carat Weight Translates to Finger Coverage

Heart shapes carry weight in the depth of the pavilion and in the spread of the lobes, which means they often look larger face-up than a round brilliant of equal carat weight. A 1.50-carat heart will typically measure around 7.5 millimeters across at its widest point — roughly comparable to the face-up diameter of a 1.30-carat round. That size-to-weight efficiency is one reason buyers drawn to romantic shapes consistently find heart shaped diamonds offer strong visual value.

On smaller hands, a stone under one carat can look proportionally ideal and less overpowering. On larger hands, the shape reads better at 1.25 carats and above. These are guidelines, not rules — the buyer's preference governs — but they reflect how the shape interacts with anatomy.

Color and clarity matter more in hearts than in rounds because the open facet structure of the upper table shows inclusions and color tints clearly. A GIA H color with a VS2 (Very Slightly Included 2) clarity grade is a reliable starting point for an eye-clean stone with near-colorless warmth. Going below I color in a heart shaped diamond without viewing the stone in person carries real risk.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What is the ideal length-to-width ratio for a heart shaped diamond?**
The sweet spot is between 0.98 and 1.02, which produces a balanced, symmetrical heart that reads clearly as a shape from a normal viewing distance. Ratios outside this range are not failures, but they require deliberate intent — a longer stone for a more elongated look, a wider stone for a bolder, fuller appearance. Ask for the exact millimeter measurements and judge against your own eye.

**Does GIA grade the cut of a heart shaped diamond?**
GIA issues cut grades only for round brilliant diamonds. For heart shapes, GIA grades polish and symmetry, both of which should be Very Good or Excellent. Cut quality — proportions, light performance, and optical symmetry — must be evaluated separately, ideally with an ASET image or by working with a jeweler who specializes in fancy shapes.

**Why does my heart shaped diamond have a dark shadow across the center?**
That is a bow-tie shadow, caused by light leaking out of the pavilion rather than returning to the eye. A faint bow-tie is normal in most fancy shapes and does not indicate a defective stone. A heavy, dark bow-tie visible across the full width of the stone indicates the pavilion angles were cut outside the optimal range, and the effect will not diminish once the stone is set.

**Is a V-prong at the point of the heart really necessary?**
Yes. The culet — the pointed tip of the stone — is the thinnest and most exposed part of a heart shaped diamond. Without a protective V-prong, even a routine knock against a hard surface can chip the point. Every heart shaped ring Bez Ambar produces includes a V-prong at the tip as standard, not as an upgrade.

**What color and clarity grades work best for a heart shaped diamond?**
Start at H color and VS2 clarity as a baseline for an eye-clean stone that shows near-colorless warmth. The open table of a heart shaped diamond displays color tints and inclusions more visibly than a round brilliant, so grades that might be acceptable in a round can be noticeable in a heart. When in doubt, view the stone in person or request high-resolution imaging before committing to a purchase.
