### What Opal Is and Why It Does What It Does
## Silica Spheres and the Physics of Color Play

Opal is not a crystal — it is amorphous silica, formed from silica-rich water that percolated through cracks and cavities in host rock and deposited in layers. The color play that makes precious opal remarkable comes from the diffraction of light through regularly arranged microscopic silica spheres. When the spheres are uniform in size and well-organized in three-dimensional arrays, they diffract different wavelengths of light differently as the viewing angle changes, producing the rolling spectral flash that the trade calls "play of color." The larger the spheres, the lower the wavelength they diffract — larger spheres produce red, smaller spheres produce blue-violet.

The quality of the color play — its intensity, the breadth of colors visible, and how they shift across the stone's face — determines value in precious opal. A stone that shows only blue and green with low brightness is a different product from one that shows vivid rolling red and orange across the full face. Ethiopian opal, Australian black opal from Lightning Ridge, and Australian crystal opal from Coober Pedy each have distinct optical characters. Black opal, where the dark body color enhances the color contrast, produces the most dramatic and valuable material.

### The Durability Problem
## Why Opal Requires a Different Conversation

Opal contains water — typically three to ten percent by weight — and this is the source of both its beauty and its vulnerability. The water content makes opal susceptible to crazing (a network of fine surface cracks that permanently damages the stone) if it dries out too rapidly due to low humidity, heat, or prolonged exposure to direct sunlight. Once a stone crazes, the damage is irreversible. Opal also has no cleavage plane, but it is relatively soft — between 5.5 and 6.5 on the Mohs scale — which means it will scratch and chip more readily than sapphire, ruby, or even tourmaline.

An opal engagement ring requires commitment to specific care practices: removing the ring during any activity that might expose it to impact, avoiding exposure to chemicals, household cleaners, and ultrasonic cleaning, and storing it appropriately to prevent dehydration. For a client with that awareness and discipline, an opal ring can be worn and maintained for decades. For a client who wants a center stone that requires no special attention, opal is the wrong choice.

### Doublets, Triplets, and Solid Opals
## Understanding What You Are Actually Buying

The opal market includes three categories of stone: solids, doublets, and triplets. Solid opals are cut directly from the natural material. Doublets are thin slices of natural opal bonded to a dark backing material — usually black potch or ironstone — to enhance color contrast. Triplets add a transparent cap of quartz or glass over the opal slice for additional protection. Doublets and triplets are less expensive and are legitimate products when fully disclosed. They become problematic when sold as solid opals or when the construction obscures value differences that the buyer would care about.

Solid opals with strong, vivid color play — particularly Lightning Ridge black opals above two carats — are among the most individually spectacular objects in the gem world. They are also among the most expensive per carat in their finest examples. A well-documented Australian black opal with full-face red fire can command prices comparable to fine ruby. Solid crystal opals and Ethiopian opals with strong color are more accessible, with excellent stones available in the $500 to $3,000 per carat range depending on the intensity and breadth of the color play.

### Setting Design for Opal
## Protection Without Sacrifice of Appearance

A bezel setting — where a metal rim encircles the circumference of the stone — is the appropriate starting point for any opal in a ring that will receive regular wear. It protects the vulnerable edge from impact, which is where chipping most often occurs. Prong settings that leave the edge of the stone exposed are higher risk in a daily-wear context, though they can work in pendant and earring applications where impact risk is lower.

The setting depth matters for doublets specifically — the seam between the opal layer and the backing must be protected from moisture infiltration, which can cause the adhesive to fail and the doublet to separate. A good jeweler who works regularly with opal will know how to set doublets with this in mind.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is Ethiopian opal as good as Australian opal?**
Ethiopian opal — primarily from the Wollo Province — has produced stones with extraordinarily vivid color play that rivals the best Australian material, and it has done so at lower price points. The traditional preference for Australian opal reflects a longer collector history and the specific character of Lightning Ridge black opal, which Ethiopian production does not replicate. Ethiopian opal can also be more susceptible to hydrophane behavior — absorbing water and temporarily losing color play, then recovering as it dries — which is worth understanding before purchase.

**Will an opal crack if I wash my hands while wearing it?**
Brief water exposure during handwashing is generally not a problem for solid opals. Prolonged submersion, exposure to hot water, and rapid temperature changes are the more significant risks. Swimming, dishwashing, and bathing are activities during which the ring should come off. The risk is not the water itself but the combination of temperature change, extended exposure, and chemical content of the water.

**Can an opal be re-polished if it scratches?**
Yes. Opal's softness means it will accumulate surface scratches more quickly than harder gemstones, but it also means re-polishing is relatively straightforward for a skilled lapidary. A scratched opal that has not crazed can often be brought back to excellent condition. Budget for occasional re-polishing over the life of a frequently worn ring.

**How do I store an opal ring?**
Store the ring in a fabric-lined box away from extreme heat, direct sunlight, and forced heating/air conditioning that creates very low humidity. Some dealers recommend storing opals with a damp cotton ball nearby in very dry environments — whether this is necessary depends on the specific stone and your climate. If the ring will be unworn for an extended period, a jeweler's advice for storage is worth seeking.

**What makes some opals so much more expensive than others?**
Body color, brilliance of the color play, and the colors represented are the primary value factors. A vivid red flash in the color play is rarest and most valued; blue-green is most common and least expensive among fine stones. Stones that show the full spectral range — red through violet — are called "harlequin" pattern and are extremely rare. Body color matters: stones with a dark or black body color show the play of color more dramatically than light or white body material.
