*The marquise cut stretches a stone's silhouette to its absolute limit — and on the right hand, nothing commands a room like it.*

### The Shape
## A Cut Born From Court, Not Commerce

The marquise diamond has a provenance that most cuts cannot claim. Louis XV of France commissioned a stone cut to mirror the lips of his mistress, the Marquise de Pompadour — an elliptical point at each end, a gentle swell at the center. That origin story is not marketing. It explains why the shape carries a weight that purely modern cuts do not. It was designed to flatter the human form, not to maximize yield from rough.

That design logic holds today. The elongated outline — jewelers call it a navette, from the French word for small boat — runs parallel to the finger, visually extending the hand. A well-proportioned marquise on a size-6 finger reads as noticeably larger than a round brilliant of identical carat weight. On a size-8 finger it reads as commanding. The effect is not an illusion so much as an honest use of geometry.

The trade misunderstands this cut more than any other. Ask the average retail jeweler to explain the marquise's appeal and you will get a shrug, a mention of its "vintage feel," and a fast pivot to the round brilliant. That pivot reveals a knowledge gap, not a market reality.

### Proportions
## The Ratio That Separates Good From Great

Every marquise diamond has a length-to-width ratio — the number you get when you divide its longest dimension by its widest. That ratio is the single most important variable in the cut. A ratio between 1.75:1 and 2.15:1 produces the classic elongated silhouette without narrowing the stone to the point where the center appears thin or the bow-tie shadow deepens into a dark canyon.

The bow-tie is the feature most buyers fear and most jewelers fail to explain honestly. It is a shadow that falls across the center of any elongated fancy shape — marquise, oval, pear — when light enters the stone at certain angles. A well-cut marquise has a bow-tie that is subtle, almost imperceptible under normal lighting. A poorly cut one looks like someone draped a black ribbon across the center of the stone. The bow-tie is not inherent to the shape. It is a consequence of cutting decisions. When a cutter sacrifices depth or deviates from proper facet angles to preserve carat weight from expensive rough, the bow-tie deepens. You are paying for that compromise.

Look at the stone under diffuse light, under point-source light, and in natural daylight before you commit. The bow-tie that disappears in one environment and reappears as a dark stripe under a ceiling spotlight is a stone with an optical problem that no setting will fix.

### Setting Choices
## The Point Protects Everything

The marquise has two pointed tips, and those tips are structurally the most vulnerable part of the stone. The crystal at each point is thin. A direct impact can chip it. This is not a reason to avoid the cut — it is a reason to set it correctly.

The standard solution is a V-prong at each tip. A properly sized V-prong cups the point without covering so much of the stone that you lose the silhouette. Bezel settings and east-west orientations — where the stone runs perpendicular to the finger — have become popular, but they change the character of the cut entirely. An east-west marquise reads as wide and bold rather than long and elegant. Neither orientation is wrong, but they are not interchangeable. Know what you want the ring to do before you choose.

### The Metal
## Yellow and White Each Tell a Different Story

Yellow gold and a marquise diamond create a warmth that platinum or white gold disrupts. The cut's vintage architecture sits naturally in yellow gold, particularly in solitaire and three-stone configurations with side baguettes. White metal sharpens the stone's apparent whiteness and suits buyers who want the cut to feel current rather than historical.

Rose gold is a legitimate third option, though it reads as softer and more romantic — appropriate for some buyers, too casual for others. The metal is not decoration around the stone. It is part of the stone's visual context, and it shapes how the cut reads at a distance.

A marquise in yellow gold at the end of a long finger is one of the most visually authoritative pieces of jewelry that exists. It earned that status over three centuries. The jeweler who dismisses it as "vintage" is the one who has not studied it.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does a marquise diamond really make your finger look longer?**
Yes, and the effect is measurable, not imagined. The elongated outline runs in the same direction as the finger, drawing the eye along that axis. A marquise with a 2:1 length-to-width ratio will make the same finger look noticeably more slender than a round or cushion cut of equal carat weight.

**What length-to-width ratio should I look for in a marquise diamond?**
The range that produces the most balanced silhouette is 1.75:1 to 2.15:1. Below 1.75, the stone looks stubby and loses most of the elongating effect. Above 2.15, the center narrows and the bow-tie shadow tends to worsen. Most buyers find the 1.85 to 2.00 range the most versatile.

**Is the bow-tie effect always present in a marquise diamond?**
Every marquise has some degree of bow-tie — it is a consequence of the geometry of elongated fancy shapes. The question is severity. A well-cut stone has a bow-tie so subtle it registers only as soft contrast, not as a dark obstruction. Always evaluate the stone under multiple lighting conditions before purchasing.

**Do V-prongs look better than bezel settings on a marquise?**
V-prongs protect the tips while preserving the full silhouette of the cut. A bezel covers more of the stone and softens the pointed ends, which changes the character of the shape significantly. If you chose a marquise for its elongated, pointed profile, a bezel undermines that choice. V-prongs are the structurally correct solution for the traditional orientation.

**Is the marquise cut considered out of style?**
Not by anyone who has looked at jewelry seriously. The marquise has cycled through fashion decades since the 18th century and never disappeared from the market. Its recent revival among buyers who want a distinctive alternative to the round brilliant reflects an understanding that distinctive cuts do not go out of style — they simply go in and out of mass-market circulation.
