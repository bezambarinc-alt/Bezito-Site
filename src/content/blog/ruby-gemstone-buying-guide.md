### Color First
## The Red That Defines a Ruby

Color is everything with rubies, and the trade knows exactly what it wants: a vivid, slightly bluish-red that the Burmese call "pigeon's blood." That phrase has become overused to the point of meaninglessness, but the underlying standard is real. The hue should be pure red with just enough blue to give it depth — not orange, not brown, and not a washed-out pink. Saturation matters as much as hue. A heavily included stone in perfect color will outperform a clean stone with flat, lifeless red at the same price point. That is a counterintuitive truth for buyers trained to prioritize clarity, and it trips up even experienced purchasers.

Pink sapphire and ruby sit on a continuum. There is no sharp line. Corundum that is definitively red — saturated, vibrant, not pastel — earns the ruby designation. Stones that read as clearly pink belong to the sapphire family for trade and pricing purposes. The difference in price between a strong ruby and a top pink sapphire of similar quality can be dramatic, so the classification is not academic. Get an independent laboratory report from GIA or Gübelin before committing to any stone above a few thousand dollars.

### Origin and Its Premium
## Why Burma, Mozambique, and Sri Lanka Matter

Ruby origin commands a price premium that is not always rational but is consistent and real. Burmese rubies — from the Mogok Valley specifically — carry the highest premiums among collectors and sophisticated buyers. The geology of Mogok produces stones with a particular fluorescence that makes them glow under daylight in a way other origins rarely match. Mozambican rubies from the Montepuez deposit have entered the market in meaningful volume over the past decade and offer strong color at lower prices than comparable Burmese material. Sri Lankan rubies tend toward lighter, more pastel tones but occasionally produce vivid material.

The premium for Burmese origin is real, but it requires documentation. A credible origin determination requires testing by a recognized laboratory — GIA, Gübelin, or SSEF — using trace-element analysis and inclusion fingerprinting. No laboratory report means no verifiable origin, which means you are paying a premium on a claim you cannot substantiate.

### Treatment: The Question That Determines Value
## Heat Treatment, Glass Filling, and What to Demand

Nearly all commercial rubies are heat-treated. The process removes silk inclusions and improves color, and it is accepted as a standard practice when disclosed. A heated ruby with strong color and a GIA report noting "indications of heating" is a perfectly legitimate stone. What changes the calculus entirely is glass filling — the practice of filling surface-reaching fractures with lead glass to improve apparent clarity and transparency. Glass-filled rubies are dramatically less durable, will be damaged by ultrasonic cleaners and re-polishing, and are worth a fraction of comparable untreated or merely heated material.

The price differential between a quality heated ruby and a comparable glass-filled stone should be enormous. If a deal seems too good, glass filling is the first explanation to investigate. Any ruby you are considering at a significant price point deserves a laboratory report that addresses treatment status explicitly.

### Price Benchmarks
## What Fine Rubies Actually Cost

Fine unheated Burmese rubies above two carats with strong color and a Gübelin or GIA report are among the most expensive gems in the world — prices at auction have exceeded $1 million per carat for exceptional material. That ceiling is not relevant to most buyers. For the working fine-jewelry market, excellent heated Burmese rubies in the one-to-two carat range trade between $8,000 and $25,000 per carat retail depending on color intensity and clarity. Mozambican material of comparable color runs meaningfully less. Below one carat, the market is broader and more accessible, but the same quality standards apply.

Do not let price alone guide you. A mediocre stone at a low price is a worse investment than a fine stone at a fair price. Rubies with genuine color, credible documentation, and honest treatment disclosure hold value. Stones that check no boxes but carry a modest price tag depreciate immediately.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is a heated ruby worth less than an unheated one?**
Heated rubies are worth less than unheated rubies of comparable quality — the premium for "no heat" can be 50 to 300 percent depending on origin and size. That said, a heated ruby with excellent color is far more valuable than an unheated stone with weak color. Treatment status matters most when color is equal.

**How do I know if a ruby is glass-filled?**
You need a laboratory report. Visual inspection under magnification can reveal gas bubbles or flash effects associated with glass filling, but confirming it definitively requires gemological testing. Never purchase a ruby above a few hundred dollars without a GIA, Gübelin, or SSEF report that addresses treatment.

**What carat weight makes sense for an engagement ring center stone?**
Most clients who come to me for ruby engagement rings are looking at stones between 0.75 and 2.5 carats. Below that range the impact is limited. Above two carats, prices escalate sharply and the quality requirements tighten considerably if you want a stone that justifies its price.

**Can rubies scratch or chip in daily wear?**
Ruby is corundum, the second hardest natural material after diamond. Scratching is not a realistic concern. Chipping is possible if the stone receives a hard blow at the right angle — the same risk exists with any gemstone including diamond. A bezel setting or a slightly recessed prong configuration reduces that risk.

**Does the shape affect value significantly?**
Rubies are most often cut in oval or cushion shapes because the rough tends to favor those forms and they preserve carat weight well. Round rubies command a modest premium for the additional cutting loss. Shape preference is personal, but for maximum color impact, the oval and cushion cuts have proved themselves over decades of production.
