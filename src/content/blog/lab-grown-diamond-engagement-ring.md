### The Science
## Lab Grown Diamonds Are the Real Thing

A lab grown diamond is not a simulant. It is not cubic zirconia. It is not moissanite. It is carbon atoms arranged in the same crystal lattice as any diamond pulled from the Kimberley mines or the Siberian tundra. Two production methods dominate the industry: High Pressure High Temperature (HPHT), which replicates the geological conditions deep in the earth, and Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), which grows the crystal layer by atom-thin layer inside a sealed reactor chamber. Both yield stones that a gemological laboratory — GIA, IGI, GCAL — grades using the same 4Cs scale applied to mined diamonds: cut, color, clarity, and carat weight. A D-VVS2 lab diamond is a D-VVS2 diamond. Full stop.

What a gemologist cannot do with standard equipment is tell a mined diamond from a lab grown one by looking at it. Specialized spectroscopic instruments can detect growth patterns, and reputable labs laser-inscribe the girdle to identify origin. Beyond that, the stones are physically, chemically, and optically identical.

### The Trade-Off
## Price Today Does Not Predict Value Tomorrow

Here is where I will be direct with you, because forty years in this business have taught me that sentiment and economics rarely move in the same direction.

Lab grown diamonds have dropped in price roughly 80 percent since 2018. The trajectory has not reversed. As reactor technology improves and production scales, the cost to grow a one-carat colorless diamond approaches the cost of manufacturing any other industrial material. That downward pressure is structural, not cyclical. A lab grown diamond purchased today at a significant discount to a mined stone will not hold resale value the way a fine natural diamond historically has. It may have little secondary market value at all within a decade.

That is not a reason to dismiss lab grown diamonds. It is a reason to be clear-eyed about what you are buying. If your priority is maximizing the visual quality of the center stone within a fixed budget — more carats, better color, higher clarity — a lab grown diamond delivers that trade-off honestly. If you are thinking of the ring as an heirloom that accumulates meaning and some residual market value across generations, the calculus changes.

### The Setting
## The Mount Matters as Much as the Stone

Every client who walks into my studio focuses almost entirely on the diamond. I understand that. The stone is the symbol. But after four decades of making custom engagement rings, I can tell you that the quality of the setting — the metalwork, the prong geometry, the way light enters the stone from below — determines how that diamond performs on the finger. A poorly mounted D-Flawless stone looks worse than a well-mounted G-VS1.

Whether you choose a mined or lab grown center stone, the mount should be fabricated, not cast. Fabricated platinum or 18-karat gold is denser, harder, and holds prongs more securely than cast metal. The difference shows over decades of daily wear. A cast prong that wore thin and lost a one-carat stone is a loss that no insurance policy fully replaces — the stone can be replaced, but the original ring cannot.

### The Decision
## What Kind of Ring Are You Making?

The engagement ring market has bifurcated, and that is not a bad thing. Some couples want the largest, most visually impressive stone their budget allows, knowing they may reset the center stone as tastes and finances change. Lab grown diamonds serve that buyer well. Other couples want a ring that is singular — a specific stone that was never duplicated, formed over billions of years, and will never be grown again. Natural diamonds serve that buyer.

Neither preference is wrong. What I resist is the framing that one choice is ethically superior to the other in all cases. Mining has environmental costs; so does industrial energy consumption in reactor facilities. Both supply chains have responsible and irresponsible operators. Certification and provenance documentation matter regardless of origin.

My recommendation is always this: decide what the ring means to you before you decide what goes in it. The best engagement ring is the one built around that meaning, executed with uncompromising craft, and designed to last longer than both of you.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Are lab grown diamonds graded the same way as natural diamonds?**
Yes. Reputable independent laboratories — GIA, IGI, and GCAL among them — apply the same 4Cs grading system to lab grown and natural diamonds. The reports look nearly identical, with the addition of a notation indicating the stone's origin. Always request a laboratory certificate regardless of which type you purchase.

**Will a lab grown diamond look different on the finger?**
No. The optical properties of a lab grown diamond — refractive index, dispersion, hardness — are identical to a natural diamond. The stone will reflect and refract light in exactly the same way. No observer can distinguish origin by looking at the ring.

**Do lab grown diamonds hold their value?**
The resale market for lab grown diamonds has declined sharply as production costs have fallen. A lab grown diamond purchased today should not be expected to retain significant resale value over time. If long-term value retention is a priority, a natural diamond from a reputable source with strong provenance documentation is the more defensible choice.

**What metal should I choose for the setting?**
Platinum is my default recommendation for engagement rings worn daily. It is harder than white gold, does not require rhodium re-plating, and develops a natural patina over decades that many clients prefer. If budget constrains the metal choice, 18-karat gold in any color is a sound alternative. I advise against 14-karat gold for engagement ring prongs — the alloy composition at that karat compromises long-term prong integrity.

**How long does a custom engagement ring take to make?**
A fully custom ring — from initial design consultation through fabrication and final setting — typically requires six to eight weeks. Clients working toward a specific date should build in additional time for any design revisions. Rush work is possible but introduces compromises I generally recommend against when the piece is meant to last a lifetime.
