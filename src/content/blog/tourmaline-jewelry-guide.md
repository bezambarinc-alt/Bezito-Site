### The Range of Tourmaline Color
## Why This Single Mineral Covers the Entire Spectrum

Tourmaline's color diversity comes from its chemical complexity. The mineral has a boron-based structure that can incorporate an unusually wide range of trace elements — copper, manganese, iron, chromium — each contributing a different color signature. The result is a single gem species that spans pink, red, blue, green, yellow, orange, brown, black, and virtually every combination between them. No other gem mineral approaches this range.

For practical purposes, the trade groups tourmaline by color variety and commercial significance. The major categories are pink and rubellite (red-pink to pure red), green (which ranges from pale mint to deep chrome-green), indicolite (blue to blue-green), watermelon (bicolored green exterior with pink interior), and Paraíba (the copper-bearing neon blue-green that commands prices comparable to fine ruby). Each category has its own price structure, treatment considerations, and collector market.

### Paraíba Tourmaline
## The Neon Blue-Green That Changed the Colored Stone Market

Paraíba tourmaline was discovered in Brazil's Paraíba state in the late 1980s and the trade has not been the same since. The copper-bearing mineral produces a blue-green of a saturation and brightness that no other gemstone replicates — a neon, almost fluorescent quality that is visible in photographs and overwhelmingly present in person. A fine Paraíba above one carat commands prices that rival fine ruby and sapphire, and exceptional material above three carats has traded at tens of thousands of dollars per carat.

Similar copper-bearing tourmalines have since been found in Nigeria and Mozambique. The trade and the laboratories have engaged in extended debate about whether "Paraíba tourmaline" should be a geographic designation or a chemical one. Currently, GIA identifies the material by origin but acknowledges the chemical similarity. African "Paraíba-type" material is generally priced below Brazilian Paraíba of equal visual quality, but the color can be indistinguishable. For buyers who want the visual experience, African material is often the more economical path.

### Rubellite and Chrome Tourmaline
## The Red and the Green That Compete Seriously

Rubellite is the trade name for tourmaline that maintains its red-pink color under both daylight and incandescent light, distinguishing it from ordinary pink tourmaline that shifts toward purple under incandescent sources. True rubellite — saturated, pure red to red-pink without browning under any light source — is considerably rarer than standard pink tourmaline and trades at a significant premium. Fine rubellite can reach per-carat prices that approach spessartine garnet and exceed most commercial sapphire.

Chrome tourmaline, colored by chromium rather than iron, produces a vivid, warm green with a slight yellow undertone that distinguishes it from the cooler, more bluish green of indicolite. Fine chrome tourmaline from Tanzania approaches the visual impression of a slightly yellower tsavorite garnet and is one of the most undervalued greens in the gem market. The stone is genuinely difficult to find in large sizes with strong saturation, and buyers who encounter it at a credible seller should take it seriously.

### Treatment and Transparency in Tourmaline
## What the Trade Does and What Buyers Should Know

Heat treatment is common in pink and red tourmaline to lighten overly dark material or improve color. The treatment is generally accepted and, for most purposes, does not carry a significant price penalty in the commercial market. Rubellite that maintains its color without heat treatment is rarer and valued accordingly, but documentation is required to make that distinction credible.

Irradiation treatment is used to produce or enhance color in some tourmalines, particularly certain pink and red varieties. It is more controversial than heat treatment and less universally disclosed. A GIA or other laboratory report will identify irradiation when it can be detected, though detection is not always possible with current technology. For significant purchases, laboratory reports are the only protection.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What makes Paraíba tourmaline so expensive?**
The copper-bearing chemistry that produces the neon blue-green color is extraordinarily rare. The original Brazilian deposit in Paraíba state has essentially been mined out of its finest material. The combination of visual impact — genuinely unlike any other gemstone — and genuine rarity drives the price. A one-carat fine Brazilian Paraíba in vivid blue-green can trade for $15,000 to $50,000 per carat. African material offers the color at better value.

**How durable is tourmaline for everyday wear?**
Tourmaline has a hardness of 7 to 7.5 on the Mohs scale, placing it in a similar range to quartz and morganite. It is harder than tanzanite but softer than sapphire. For daily wear rings, tourmaline works well in protective settings. It does not have cleavage issues like topaz, which means it is less vulnerable to splitting from impact than some other mid-hardness stones.

**Can I tell watermelon tourmaline apart from synthetic material?**
Watermelon tourmaline — crystals that show a green rind and pink core — is not synthetically produced commercially. The visual effect you see in a watermelon slice cross-section is entirely natural, and the stone does not need treatment to produce the color zoning. Synthetic tourmaline is not currently a commercial product to worry about in the same way synthetic ruby or emerald is.

**What size is reasonable for tourmaline center stones?**
Tourmaline is frequently available in large sizes because the crystals grow large and the cutting is often done for color rather than maximum weight yield. Stones of five to twenty carats are not unusual in the pink, green, and bicolor categories, and the pricing makes large tourmalines accessible. Paraíba is the exception — significant fine Paraíba above three carats is extremely rare.

**Does tourmaline require any special handling during cleaning?**
Tourmaline is generally stable for cleaning in warm soapy water with a soft brush. Avoid rapid temperature changes and prolonged steam cleaning. Ultrasonic cleaning is acceptable for tourmaline without surface fractures. Irradiated tourmaline may fade under prolonged, intense ultraviolet exposure — avoid leaving such stones in direct strong sunlight for extended periods.
