*Jewelry stored carelessly in a pile does not get stolen — it damages itself, piece by piece, every time a harder stone contacts a softer one or a prong tip catches on a neighboring chain.*

### The Damage
## How Improper Storage Destroys Jewelry Over Time

Diamond is the hardest natural substance on earth, with a Mohs hardness of 10. Every other gemstone and all precious metals are softer. This means that a loose diamond, or any diamond-set piece, can scratch any other piece of jewelry it comes into contact with. A diamond ring stored in contact with an emerald will eventually scratch that emerald's surface. A diamond bracelet stored in contact with a pearl necklace will destroy the pearl's nacre. This is not gradual or theoretical damage — it is real and happens at every point of stone-on-stone or stone-on-metal contact.

Gold, platinum, and silver all scratch each other. The Mohs hardnesses are different (platinum is harder than gold), but both are in the range where contact between pieces in storage produces visible surface damage over time. A high-polish gold bracelet stored in a tangle with other pieces will develop a network of fine scratches across its surface — each individual scratch too small to see clearly but together producing a hazy, diffuse surface appearance that replaces the original mirror finish.

Pearl is the most vulnerable material in a jewelry collection. Natural and cultured pearls have a surface nacre composed of aragonite platelets — the same mineral as calcium carbonate — with a hardness around 2.5 on the Mohs scale. Contact with any metal object, let alone a diamond or sapphire, will scratch nacre. Pearls should always be stored in a separate soft pouch, away from any piece set with stones or hard metal edges. A pearl necklace stored in a glass bowl with other pieces will be visibly damaged within a year.

### The Solution
## How to Organize and Store a Fine Jewelry Collection

Individual compartmentalization is the organizing principle of proper jewelry storage. Each piece should have its own space — whether that is a dedicated slot in a lined jewelry box, its own small fabric pouch, or a separate compartment in a travel roll. Pieces that do not contact each other cannot damage each other. This principle scales from a travel roll carrying three pieces to a full jewelry cabinet holding thirty.

The interior lining material matters. Velvet is the traditional jewelry box lining and remains the best choice for stationary storage. It is soft enough to prevent surface scratches, provides enough friction to keep pieces from sliding, and does not shed fibers that can become entangled in fine chain links. Satin and silk linings are aesthetically pleasing but more slippery, allowing pieces to migrate against each other when a drawer is opened or closed. Felt is adequate but rougher at microscopic surface levels than velvet.

Necklace and bracelet storage requires particular attention because chain entanglement is the other major storage damage mechanism. A chain that has knotted itself will sustain mechanical stress at the knot when you try to unknot it, and a fine chain knotted tightly enough can break at that stress point. Store necklaces hanging — from hooks in a cabinet, from a wall-mounted bar, or suspended from individual pins in a lined drawer. For pieces that must be stored flat, each piece in its own sealed pouch prevents entanglement with its neighbors.

### The Environment
## Temperature, Humidity, and Light as Storage Variables

Most gemstones and metals are stable across normal room temperature ranges, but some materials require more care. Pearls need humidity — they are organic material, and extended storage in a very dry environment causes the nacre to dry out and eventually crack. Storing pearls in a sealed airtight container in a very dry climate can cause this damage. A slightly humid environment, or occasional brief exposure to air, maintains pearl health.

Opals are similarly sensitive to environmental extremes. Opal contains water as part of its mineral structure, and significant dehydration causes the opal to craze — develop a network of fine surface cracks that permanently damages the stone's play of color. Avoid storing opal jewelry in direct sunlight, in heating vents, or in very low humidity environments.

Colored gemstones should be stored away from direct sunlight for extended periods. Ultraviolet exposure will fade some stones — amethyst, kunzite, and rose quartz are the most common examples — over months or years of consistent light exposure. A jewelry box or drawer in a location without direct sun exposure is sufficient protection. Diamonds are not affected by ultraviolet exposure.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is a home safe the right place to store fine jewelry?**
A home safe provides theft deterrence but is not necessarily ideal from a material-care standpoint. A fireproof home safe can reach very high temperatures in a house fire despite its fire rating — the temperature that protects paper may still damage certain gemstones and metals. If using a safe for fire protection, ensure pieces are in individual pouches and that the safe carries an adequate fire rating. For primary security, a bank safe deposit box is the most secure option.

**How should I store a tennis necklace or bracelet overnight?**
A tennis necklace or bracelet should be stored flat and uncoiled in a lined compartment or on a soft flat surface. Do not fold or compress these pieces, because each fold point stresses the link hinges. A flat storage position distributes the piece's weight evenly and prevents the hinges from developing a permanent set in one direction.

**Can I store multiple rings on a ring holder together?**
A ring holder or ring roll that keeps rings separated with fabric between each ring is acceptable storage. Rings stored on a bare metal rod in direct contact with each other will scratch each other and potentially scratch their stones if any of them carry diamonds. The fabric separation between rings on a soft ring roll prevents this contact.

**Is humidity a problem for diamond and gold jewelry?**
Standard room humidity is not a problem for diamonds or precious metals. High sustained humidity — above 70% relative humidity — can cause tarnishing on silver-alloy metals and should be avoided for extended storage. Most fine jewelry in 14k or 18k gold is not significantly affected by normal home humidity ranges.

**What is the best way to store jewelry I am not wearing regularly?**
Pieces not in active rotation should be cleaned before storage (contamination left on stones can etch some softer stone surfaces over very long storage periods), individually pouched in acid-free fabric or tissue, placed in compartmentalized storage, and documented with current photographs. If stored in a bank safe deposit box, include a copy of the appraisal documentation in the box.
