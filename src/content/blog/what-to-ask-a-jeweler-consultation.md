### The Stone Questions
## What to Ask About the Diamond or Gemstone

The first question to ask about any diamond is whether it comes with a laboratory grading report, and if so, from which laboratory. The Gemological Institute of America (GIA) and the American Gem Society (AGS) are the two most respected independent grading laboratories for diamonds. A diamond with a GIA or AGS grading report has been assessed by a third party with no commercial interest in the grade outcome. A diamond graded in-house, or with a report from an unrecognized laboratory, carries grades that are only as reliable as the entity issuing them.

Ask about the stone's origin if origin matters to you. Natural diamonds from the earth, laboratory-grown diamonds, and diamond simulants are three different materials at different price points. Any seller who is not immediately transparent about which type you are looking at is not operating to an acceptable standard of disclosure. The same applies to colored stones — natural, laboratory-created, and treated stones must be disclosed, and the treatment type must be specified. Asking "Is this stone natural, and has it been treated?" and receiving an evasive or vague answer tells you what you need to know about the seller.

For colored stones in the fine jewelry category, ask about the stone's origin if it is relevant to the price. A Burma ruby of no-heat-treatment status from a major laboratory commands a price premium over a heat-treated Thai ruby of similar apparent appearance. If the price is at the premium level, the documentation should confirm the premium characteristics. Ask for the specific laboratory report, not just a verbal assurance.

### The Metal Questions
## What to Confirm About the Setting Material

Ask about metal karat and confirm it is marked on the piece. In the United States, any gold jewelry sold as "gold" must carry a karat stamp — 14K, 18K, 750, 585 — either on the piece itself or on a tag securely attached to it. An unstamped piece sold as gold is a regulatory compliance issue and a quality red flag. Platinum pieces are stamped PLAT, PT950, or similar. Confirm the stamp before purchase.

Ask whether white gold will require rhodium plating and if so, what the expected frequency is. This is a maintenance cost that buyers frequently discover after purchase, and it should be disclosed upfront. White gold is an alloy that has a yellow tone in its natural state; rhodium plating creates the white surface. The plating wears at a rate determined by wear frequency and the wearer's skin chemistry. A seller who does not mention this when selling white gold is withholding relevant cost information.

Ask what happens if the metal needs repair — who performs the service, what the turnaround time is, and whether the repair is covered under any warranty. A piece with no stated service policy is a piece with no accountability after the sale.

### The Service Questions
## What to Ask About After-Sale Accountability

Ask whether the piece is covered by any warranty, and what specifically the warranty covers. Fine jewelry warranties from reputable makers typically cover manufacturing defects — a prong that was improperly formed, a stone that was loose from improper setting — but do not cover normal wear, accidental damage, or loss. A warranty that covers "everything" with no qualification should prompt follow-up questions about what "everything" actually means.

Ask whether the jeweler provides professional inspection and service after purchase, and at what cost. Annual inspection is the most important maintenance commitment in fine jewelry ownership. A jeweler who will not service what they sell — or who charges rates that make annual service cost-prohibitive — is not a long-term partner in that piece's care. Ask specifically whether cleaning and inspection are available at the studio and what the fee is.

Ask about the return and exchange policy before finalizing any purchase. Reputable jewelers have clearly stated policies. A no-returns policy is common for custom pieces — that is reasonable because a custom piece cannot be restocked. A no-returns policy on non-custom pieces without clear disclosure of that policy at the time of purchase is worth noting. A jeweler who becomes evasive or defensive when asked about returns before a sale has told you something you need to know.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What should I say when I feel pressured during a jewelry consultation?**
Say you need time to think and would like to come back. A jeweler who applies pressure tactics — limited-time offers, urgency language, discouragement of comparison shopping — is telling you they are not confident the piece will sell on its own merits. Fine jewelry purchases are significant financial and personal decisions. A jeweler who respects that will never pressure you to decide on a timeline that serves them rather than you.

**Is it appropriate to ask to see a diamond loose before it is set?**
Yes, and a reputable jeweler will accommodate this request for any significant stone. Viewing a diamond loose — before it is set — allows you to examine the stone from angles that a setting restricts, confirm the laboratory grade against your own observation, and compare it against other stones at similar grade points. We show clients every stone we are proposing for their commission in person before any fabrication begins.

**What documentation should I receive with a fine jewelry purchase?**
At minimum: a written description of the piece including metal type and karat, stone type and approximate quality, and total weight; a laboratory grading report for any diamond above 0.50 carats; and an invoice or receipt stating the purchase price. Ideally also: an insurance replacement appraisal, photographs of the piece, and any warranty documentation. If a seller is unwilling to provide a written description and receipt, do not complete the purchase.

**How do I evaluate whether a jeweler is technically qualified?**
Ask about formal training. A Graduate Gemologist (GG) from the Gemological Institute of America is the most recognized credential in the industry. American Gem Society (AGS) credentials are also respected. Bench experience matters as much as credentials — ask how long the studio has been in operation and whether fabrication is done in-house or outsourced. A jeweler who fabricates in-house has direct knowledge of every piece and full control over the quality of the work.

**Can I bring my own stone to a jeweler for a custom setting?**
Yes, and most reputable jewelers will accommodate this with one important caveat: they will evaluate the stone's condition, dimensions, and setting compatibility before committing to the work. A stone that is chipped, improperly proportioned for the intended setting, or presents a security risk in certain setting styles needs to be discussed before fabrication begins. We accept client-provided stones for custom settings and provide a written assessment of any concerns before proceeding.
