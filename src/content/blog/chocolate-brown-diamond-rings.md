*Brown diamonds were once sorted out of gem-quality inventory. Rebranded as chocolate and champagne in the 1990s, they became a legitimate jewelry category with a genuine aesthetic identity.*

---

For most of the twentieth century, brown diamonds were directed toward industrial uses: drill bits, abrasives, grinding tools. The color was considered a defect. There was no consumer market for a brown diamond because no one had built one.

In 1996, the Argyle mine in Australia began marketing its substantial inventory of brown diamonds under trade names: "chocolate" for the deepest brown-orange tones, "champagne" for lighter cognac and honey colors. The strategy worked. Brown diamonds are now a genuine segment of the colored diamond market, with their own price points, grading conventions, and aesthetic identity.

### How the Color
## Forms

Brown color in diamonds results from plastic deformation of the crystal lattice during formation. Intense heat and pressure deep in the earth can distort the regular arrangement of carbon atoms, creating structural defects that absorb certain wavelengths of light and produce a brown or orange-brown color.

This differs from the nitrogen impurities that cause yellow color or the boron that causes blue. Brown color is not the result of a foreign element but of physical stress on the lattice itself. This distinction has no practical consequence for the buyer, but it explains why some diamonds show both yellow and brown tones: they can have nitrogen impurities alongside structural deformation.

The depth of the brown color varies considerably. Light champagne reads almost golden under warm light. Cognac is a medium amber. Deep chocolate is a rich dark brown with minimal orange undertone. The trade names have no standardized gemological meaning, but they loosely correspond to light, medium, and deep brown intensities.

### Pairing Brown Diamonds
## with Metal

Brown diamonds have a warmth that positions them naturally with warm metals. The relationship between a chocolate brown diamond and rose gold is one of the most effective pairings in contemporary colored diamond jewelry: the copper warmth of the metal harmonizes with the earth tones of the stone in a way that reads organic and considered.

Yellow gold creates a similar warmth with a slightly more traditional resonance. The deep cognac and chocolate tones of a brown diamond against yellow gold have historical precedent in Victorian jewelry, where warm stones and warm metals were paired routinely.

White gold and platinum work differently against a brown diamond: the cool, bright metal creates contrast with the warm stone and can make the brown color read more distinctly by isolating it. Whether you want the metal to harmonize or contrast is a design decision, not a quality consideration.

### Value
## and Honesty

Brown diamonds cost significantly less per carat than white diamonds of equivalent size and cut quality. A 1.00-carat Fancy Intense brown diamond might be priced at $1,500 to $4,000 depending on the specific color depth and any secondary hues. A 1.00-carat white diamond of equivalent size, cut, and clarity would typically be $5,000 to $10,000 or more.

This price differential is real, and it is worth being clear-eyed about. A brown diamond is a less rare stone than a white one. Its rebranding as "chocolate" or "champagne" is a marketing strategy, not a geological discovery. The stone's appeal should rest on its actual aesthetic qualities, not an inflated sense of rarity.

That said, the aesthetic qualities are genuine. A well-chosen deep brown diamond in rose gold has a specific warmth and organic quality that white diamonds do not have. If that is the look you want, the price point makes it very accessible.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Are chocolate diamonds the same as brown diamonds?**
Yes. "Chocolate diamond" is a trade name used primarily by Le Vian, which trademarked the term. All chocolate diamonds are brown diamonds; not all brown diamonds are trademarked as chocolate. The color category is the same regardless of the trade name used.

**Do brown diamonds hold their value?**
Less well than white diamonds or high-intensity colored diamonds. Brown diamonds are the most common colored diamonds and command the lowest per-carat prices in the fancy color market. They are best purchased for the specific look they offer rather than as an investment.

**What does GIA say about chocolate diamonds?**
GIA grades brown diamonds using the same colored diamond intensity scale: Fancy Light, Fancy, Fancy Intense, Fancy Deep. A GIA report will describe the color as "brown" or "brownish-orange" or similar depending on the specific hue. The trade names chocolate and champagne do not appear on GIA reports.

**Is a brown diamond durable?**
Yes. All diamonds are 10 on the Mohs hardness scale regardless of color. The structural deformation that causes brown color does not meaningfully affect durability for jewelry purposes. Brown diamonds are appropriate for daily wear.

**What clarity grade should I look for in a brown diamond?**
The deep body color of brown diamonds conceals inclusions more effectively than white diamonds. An SI1 or even SI2 clarity grade can read clean to the naked eye in a deeper chocolate brown, whereas the same grade in a white diamond might show visible inclusions. This means you can often accept a lower clarity grade in a brown diamond without affecting the appearance.
