*Sizing a ring is one of the most common jeweler's services requested. What actually happens in the process, and when it is not possible, is worth knowing before you need it.*

---

Most people have had a ring resized at some point. Fewer understand what actually happens during the process, which metals size easily versus which resist, and the specific situations where a resizing request will be turned down by any competent jeweler because the construction of the ring makes it genuinely impossible.

Knowing this in advance prevents a common disappointment: buying a ring that cannot be resized and only discovering it when the ring no longer fits.

### Sizing Up
## Adding Material

To make a ring larger, a jeweler cuts the shank at the bottom, pulls the two ends apart to create the needed gap, and adds a piece of matching metal to fill it. The addition is soldered in place and then the seam is finished and polished until it is invisible.

The process sounds simple and largely is, with the right materials. Yellow gold and rose gold in 14k or 18k are the most cooperative. They solder cleanly, the colors match well, and the finished seam is essentially undetectable to the naked eye.

White gold is slightly more complex because the added metal needs to match the rhodium-plated finish of the surrounding shank. The seam may be visible before replating but disappears after the rhodium is applied to the entire shank surface.

Platinum sizes very well: the metal is welded rather than soldered (different heat and technique) but the result is a seamless addition.

### Sizing Down
## Removing Material

To make a ring smaller, a jeweler cuts out a small section of shank, joins the two ends, solders them closed, and finishes the seam. This is slightly easier than sizing up because no new material is introduced.

The same metals that size up well size down well. The same limitations that apply in one direction apply in both.

One additional consideration when sizing down: removing metal from the shank changes the shank's thickness slightly, particularly if the original shank is thin. Multiple rounds of downsizing can eventually weaken the shank if too much material is removed. A jeweler will advise when a shank is approaching the limit of further reduction.

### When Sizing
## Is Not Possible

Certain ring constructions cannot be sized without destroying what makes them what they are.

Eternity bands, where stones run continuously all the way around the shank, are the most common example. Cutting the shank to add or remove material necessarily destroys the continuous stone setting in that section. There is no clean way to add or remove metal without gaps or disruption in the stone pattern.

Tungsten carbide and titanium rings cannot be sized at all. These metals cannot be soldered with standard jewelry techniques; they require specialized machine tools to work with and the result is not a seamless, invisible repair. Many jewelers will not attempt it. These metals are not appropriate for rings that may need resizing.

Rings with delicate or elaborate surface decoration, such as milgrain edge patterns or engraving, are harder to resize because the sizing operation affects the surrounding area and the pattern may not match seamlessly after repair.

### The Invisible
## Sizing Adjuster

For rings that cannot be resized in the conventional way, or for wearers whose finger size fluctuates significantly across seasons, a sizing adjuster is an alternative. A small guard, made of metal or plastic, is added to the inner surface of the shank to effectively reduce the interior diameter without altering the ring itself. These are removable and adjustable.

They are not a substitute for a correctly sized ring in terms of comfort or aesthetics, but they provide a workable solution for eternity bands or other rings where conventional resizing is not possible.

### The Right Size
## from the Start

The most reliable solution is getting the ring sized correctly before it is made, or ordered at a size that allows for easy resizing within one or two steps after delivery. For engagement rings and wedding bands specifically, sizing is worth taking seriously before purchase because the alternative, resizing after the fact, is an additional step and expense that is avoidable.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How many sizes can a ring be resized?**
Most rings can be resized by up to two full sizes up or down without issue. Beyond that range, the amount of metal being added or removed begins to affect the structural integrity or the visual proportions of the ring. The specific limit depends on the original ring's construction and metal weight.

**How long does resizing take?**
A standard resize with a reputable jeweler takes one to three business days. Rush service is often available at an additional cost. For rings with significant stone settings or unusual construction, more time may be required to assess and complete the work carefully.

**Does resizing damage a ring?**
A properly executed resize by a skilled jeweler leaves no visible evidence of the work. The seam is polished smooth and the finish is restored. A poorly executed resize can leave a visible seam or affect the ring's finish in the area of the work. Ask about a jeweler's resizing experience before entrusting significant pieces.

**Can a ring with gemstones all around it be resized?**
Not without disruption to the setting. Eternity bands with stones continuously around the shank cannot be conventionally resized. Half-eternity bands, where stones cover only the top half of the visible shank, can sometimes be sized by cutting in the plain section at the bottom. Confirm before purchasing any ring with extensive stone coverage that it can be resized to what you need.

**What is the invisible sizing technique?**
Some jewelers offer a technique where the inside of the shank is built up slightly with additional metal, reducing the interior diameter without any external evidence of the work. This is appropriate for small adjustments, typically half a size. It does not involve cutting the shank and is useful for rings where shank integrity is critical.
