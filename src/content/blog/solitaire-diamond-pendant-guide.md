*A single diamond hanging from a fine chain is not the easy choice — it is the unforgiving one, where quality of stone and precision of setting determine everything.*

---

### The Stone
## Why the Diamond Does All the Work

Remove all supporting stones, remove pave, remove any decorative metalwork that draws the eye, and what remains is one diamond and the chain it hangs from. That simplicity is the appeal and the challenge. Every characteristic of that stone — color, clarity, cut quality, proportions — is visible to anyone who looks. There is no visual noise to soften a lower-clarity inclusion or warm the color of a stone that grades near the boundary between G and H.

This is why the solitaire pendant is where cut quality matters most. A well-cut diamond returns light from the center outward in a symmetrical, intense pattern. A stone with poor proportions — a table that is too large, a pavilion that is too shallow — leaks light through the base and looks flat. You will see the difference at a glance. When a client asks me which stone attributes to prioritize for a solitaire pendant, the answer is always cut first, then color, then clarity. Cut is the variable most within human control at the cutting wheel, and it determines how the stone behaves in every lighting condition the wearer encounters.

Shape is a genuinely open choice. Round brilliants maximize light return and have the broadest resale market, but oval cuts at the same carat weight appear larger face-up because the elongated silhouette covers more surface area. Pear shapes point downward from the bail and have a directional elegance that some clients find more personal than a round. Marquise cuts are the most effective at appearing large for their weight, which matters when budget and visual impact are both constraints. Each shape has a right answer only in the context of the person wearing it.

### The Setting
## Four Prongs, Bezel, or Something in Between

A solitaire pendant setting serves two functions: it secures the stone and it presents the stone. The classic four-prong setting lifts the diamond off the metal base, allowing light to enter the stone from the sides and exit through the top facets in full. It is an elegant, time-tested solution. The prong tips are the only part of a four-prong setting that require periodic inspection — they wear at the contact points and need retipping before a tip breaks.

A bezel setting wraps a continuous wall of metal around the girdle of the diamond. It provides better impact protection than prongs, which matters for wearers who are physically active or work with their hands. The tradeoff is a slight reduction in light entering from the sides. Modern bezel settings can be cut very thin — we often work in 0.3mm to 0.5mm walls — which minimizes the light reduction while retaining the protective advantage.

A partial bezel, or semi-bezel, covers the stone at two opposing points and leaves the sides open. This is a useful middle position: more protection than four prongs, more light than a full bezel. It also reads as a more modern silhouette than either of the traditional options.

### The Chain
## The Piece Nobody Thinks About Until It Breaks

A solitaire pendant is only as good as the chain and bail it hangs from. The bail — the loop or frame that connects the pendant to the chain — must be proportionate to the pendant. An undersized bail looks like an afterthought and can stress the chain at the connection point. An oversized bail draws attention away from the stone.

The chain itself should have sufficient weight to hang straight without pulling the pendant sideways, and sufficient flexibility to drape cleanly at the collarbone. Fine box chains in 0.8mm to 1.0mm diameter provide a nearly invisible suspension that makes the diamond appear to float. Slightly heavier cable chains in 1.2mm to 1.5mm are more durable for everyday wear and handle the mechanics of re-clasping better over years of use. Rope chains add texture and are visually heavier, which creates a different visual balance with the pendant stone.

On chain length: sixteen inches positions the pendant at the base of the neck, close to the collarbone. Eighteen inches allows more movement and a more relaxed placement. Sixteen or seventeen inches is the right choice for pendants that are the focal point; eighteen or longer works better for layering. The pendant should not compete with the clavicle bone — it should sit just at or below the collarbone for the most flattering placement on most body types.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What carat weight is most practical for a solitaire pendant?**
For everyday wear, 0.50 to 1.00 carat in a round or oval brilliant delivers clear visual presence without excessive weight on a delicate chain. Below 0.50 carat, the stone can disappear at wearing distance. Above 1.50 carats, the pendant may need a heavier chain and bail to hang straight, which changes the character of the piece from delicate to substantial. The right answer depends entirely on the wearer's frame and how they intend to wear it.

**Does the pendant bail matter for resale value?**
The bail does not affect gemological value, but it affects the piece's longevity and rewearability. A poorly designed bail stresses the chain at the junction point and eventually cracks. A bail with a generous interior diameter accepts standard chain gauges, so the chain can be replaced independently of the pendant. We design our bails to accommodate at least two common chain widths.

**Can I use a solitaire pendant stone for a ring later?**
The stone, yes. The pendant setting, no — ring settings are fabricated to withstand lateral impact from all directions, while pendant settings are not. The stone itself can be removed and reset in a ring setting if the shape and size are appropriate. We have done this many times for clients whose circumstances changed after purchase.

**What metal is best for a solitaire pendant?**
Platinum is the most durable and requires no surface maintenance beyond polishing. 18k white gold is lighter and less expensive, but requires rhodium plating every two to three years to maintain its white color. 18k yellow gold and rose gold require no plating and develop a natural patina over time that many clients prefer. The choice is personal; none is structurally inferior for pendant use.

**How do I choose between a round and an oval diamond for a pendant?**
At equal carat weight, an oval appears larger face-up due to its elongated shape. If visual size per dollar is the priority, the oval wins. If maximum light return and the most recognized silhouette are the priority, the round brilliant wins. I ask clients to look at both shapes in the same carat weight and lighting before deciding — the preference that emerges from that comparison is almost always the correct one.
