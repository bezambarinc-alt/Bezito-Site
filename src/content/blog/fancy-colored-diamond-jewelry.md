*Colored diamonds are graded on a different scale than white diamonds. Understanding that scale is the first step to making sense of what you are actually buying.*

---

A white diamond's value decreases as color increases: the D-to-Z color scale treats anything toward Z as a liability. For colored diamonds, the logic inverts entirely. A diamond with strong, saturated yellow color is worth more than a pale yellow one. A vivid pink commands a price that defies comparison to any white diamond of equivalent size.

These are different markets with different physics. A colored diamond's value comes from the rarity and intensity of its color, not from the absence of it. Learning to think about them on their own terms is necessary before any purchasing decision makes sense.

### The Color
## Spectrum

Colored diamonds occur across a broad spectrum. Yellow is the most common, which is why it is also the most accessible in terms of price. Moving up in rarity: orange, pink, blue, green, and red. Red diamonds are among the rarest gemstones on earth, with fewer than thirty to forty true red diamonds known to exist in finished jewelry.

The hue alone does not determine value. The modifier matters. A yellowish-orange diamond is different from an orange diamond with yellowish overtones. GIA grades colored diamonds for hue, tone, and saturation, and the specific combination of those factors produces the final grade.

Color in diamonds comes from different sources. Yellow results from nitrogen atoms displacing carbon in the crystal lattice. Blue comes from boron. Pink and red result from plastic deformation of the crystal structure during formation. Green results from natural irradiation over geological time. Each color has its own geological story.

### How GIA
## Grades Color

GIA's colored diamond grading scale uses nine intensity levels: Faint, Very Light, Light, Fancy Light, Fancy, Fancy Intense, Fancy Vivid, Fancy Deep, and Fancy Dark. From Fancy Light onward are the grades that appear in engagement ring and jewelry purchasing decisions.

Fancy Light yellow is the entry point for stones that show meaningful color. Fancy yellow is more saturated. Fancy Intense yellow is where the color becomes genuinely striking. Fancy Vivid yellow sits at the top of the scale: saturated, pure, and priced accordingly.

This gradient applies across all hues. A Fancy Intense pink is substantially more saturated than a Fancy Light pink, and the price difference between them is not linear. A small jump in color saturation at the high end represents a significant jump in rarity and therefore price.

### How Colored Diamonds
## Behave in Light

White diamonds are evaluated partly on how they return light: the quality of their fire and brilliance as the stone moves. Colored diamonds behave differently.

A colored diamond's body color is most visible in diffused, natural light. Under very bright direct light, the body color can be overwhelmed by white light return. Under softer light, the color saturates and reads most powerfully. This is why colored diamond jewelry can look dramatically different in different environments.

The cut of a colored diamond is also optimized differently. For white diamonds, cutting maximizes light return. For colored diamonds, cutting maximizes color saturation, often meaning deeper pavilion angles and larger table facets than a white diamond cutter would use. A cut that would be considered suboptimal for a white diamond may be ideal for a colored one.

### What Colored Diamonds
## Cost

Yellow diamonds are the most accessible. A Fancy Intense yellow in 1.00 carat can range from $5,000 to $15,000 depending on specific color, cut, and clarity. A Fancy Vivid yellow at the same size can reach $15,000 to $40,000 or more.

Pink diamonds are significantly more expensive. The closure of the Argyle mine in Australia in 2020 removed the world's primary source of pink diamonds from the supply chain. Prices have increased substantially since.

Blue, green, and red diamonds trade at auction-level prices. A Fancy Vivid blue in 1.00 carat is a multi-million-dollar stone. These are investment-grade assets that happen to be set into jewelry.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Are colored diamonds real diamonds?**
Yes. Colored diamonds are chemically identical to white diamonds: pure carbon in a cubic crystal structure. The color comes from trace elements or structural anomalies in the crystal, not from any additive or coating. GIA grades both white and colored diamonds as diamonds.

**What is the difference between a natural colored diamond and a lab-grown colored diamond?**
Natural colored diamonds formed over billions of years under geological conditions. Lab-grown colored diamonds are produced in controlled environments in weeks. Both are chemically identical. The market treats them very differently: natural colored diamonds at higher intensity grades command significant premiums over lab-grown equivalents.

**Are yellow diamonds worth buying as an investment?**
Fancy Vivid yellow diamonds from reputable sources have historically held value, but the colored diamond market is less liquid than the white diamond market. Selling a colored diamond requires finding a specialized buyer. For most people, colored diamonds should be purchased because you want to wear them, with investment value as a secondary consideration.

**What metal works best with yellow diamonds?**
Yellow gold maximizes the warmth of a yellow diamond and creates a harmonious composition. White gold and platinum provide contrast that can make the color read more clearly against the metal. Rose gold adds warmth without the direct tonal match of yellow gold. All three are appropriate; the choice depends on your preference for harmony versus contrast.

**Can I get a GIA certificate for a colored diamond?**
Yes. GIA grades colored diamonds and produces a report specifying the color grade, including hue, tone, saturation, and intensity level, as well as clarity and fluorescence. A GIA certificate is the standard for establishing that a colored diamond is natural and accurately graded. Never purchase a significant colored diamond without one.
