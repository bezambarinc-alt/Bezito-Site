*A piece of fine jewelry that is never serviced will eventually fail — a prong will break, a clasp will give way, or a stone will fall out — and the cost of that failure is always higher than the cost of the annual inspection that would have prevented it.*

---

### The Inspection
## What a Professional Annual Inspection Covers and Why Each Item Matters

An annual professional inspection of fine jewelry covers five areas: prong integrity, stone security, metal condition, clasp function, and surface finish. Each of these areas has a specific failure mode that regular inspection catches before it becomes a loss event.

Prong integrity inspection checks the condition of prong tips across every stone in the piece. Prong tips are the metal points that fold over the stone's girdle and hold it in place. They are the smallest, highest-wearing elements in any fine jewelry setting. Prong tips experience friction from fabric, contact from neighboring objects, and the mechanical stress of daily flexion. A prong tip that has worn thin will fail suddenly under any of these forces, releasing the stone. The inspection catches thin prong tips and allows them to be retipped before that failure occurs. This is a straightforward bench operation that costs twenty to forty dollars per prong and prevents stone loss that would cost many times that to remedy.

Stone security testing checks each stone's stability in its setting. The bench jeweler applies gentle lateral pressure to each stone using a wooden or soft plastic tool and confirms that the stone does not rock or shift. A stone that moves in its setting has a loose seating that will worsen with continued wear. Catching a loose stone during inspection means tightening it in place; discovering a loose stone on the floor means replacing it from inventory or sourcing a matched replacement — a significantly more expensive outcome.

Metal condition inspection looks for cracks, thinning, deformation, and other structural issues in the metal components of the piece. Ring shanks thin at the bottom from friction against hard surfaces. Bracelet links develop stress at hinge points from repeated flexion. Necklace chain links can develop cracks at solder joints from accumulated mechanical fatigue. Catching metal thinning before it becomes a break is the difference between a solder repair and a structural reconstruction.

### The Cleaning
## Professional Cleaning Versus Home Cleaning and Why Both Matter

Professional cleaning is part of the annual service visit and produces results that home cleaning cannot match. Steam cleaning at professional temperatures removes contamination from inside settings, beneath stones, and inside chain links that a brush and warm water cannot reach. Ultrasonic cleaning at controlled frequencies dislodges hardened contamination from prong junctions and setting corners without mechanical contact. After both, a final polish with professional polishing compounds restores the metal surface to near-original condition.

Home cleaning is still necessary between annual professional service visits. The contamination cycle described in a separate post on home cleaning — body oil, lotion, soap film accumulating on stone surfaces — occurs within days of a professional cleaning. A diamond ring cleaned professionally in January and not touched again until January will be heavily contaminated by March. Home cleaning maintains the stone's optical performance between professional visits. Professional cleaning resets the baseline.

Pieces that should receive professional inspection more than once per year include any ring worn daily in an active environment, tennis bracelets, any piece with pave or micro-pave settings, and any piece that has experienced an impact or unusual stress event. Pave and micro-pave settings involve very small prong tips or beaded prongs in high volume, and the individual tips are small enough that wear can proceed below visibility before the failure event. Semi-annual inspection of heavily pave-set pieces is the responsible standard.

### The Schedule
## Building a Maintenance Calendar for a Fine Jewelry Collection

The maintenance calendar for a fine jewelry collection should be organized by piece type and wear frequency. Daily-wear pieces — engagement rings, wedding bands, everyday earrings — should be inspected annually. Occasionally-worn pieces — statement pieces worn monthly or less — can be inspected every two to three years. Never-worn pieces should still be inspected every three to five years because metal and stone conditions can change in storage.

The best time to schedule annual jewelry inspection is either at the beginning of the calendar year — a fresh start that makes the appointment easy to remember — or immediately before or after any significant event at which the piece will be worn. A ring inspected two weeks before a destination wedding or a significant anniversary trip is one where any problem is identified in time to be corrected rather than discovered when the piece fails at the event.

Keep a maintenance log for each significant piece. Note the date of each inspection, the findings, and any work performed. This log serves multiple purposes: it confirms to an insurance company that the piece was maintained in good condition, it provides a history that helps a new jeweler understand what previous service has been performed, and it surfaces patterns — a prong that needs retipping every two years on the same ring suggests that a prong redesign might be more economical than continuous retipping.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How long does an annual jewelry inspection take?**
A routine inspection of a single piece takes thirty to forty-five minutes at the bench. If any remedial work is identified — prong retipping, stone tightening, minor metal repair — the piece may need to stay for a day or two. A collection of five to ten pieces inspected in a single appointment typically requires leaving the pieces for a half day to a full day. Call ahead to schedule a dedicated appointment rather than walking in.

**Can I do any part of the inspection myself at home?**
A basic home check is useful as a supplement to professional inspection, not a replacement. You can inspect your own prong tips by looking at them with good lighting and a magnifier — prong tips should be rounded and even, not sharp, flattened, or missing. You can check stone security by pressing gently on each stone and listening for any movement or clicking. Any stone that moves or any prong that looks worn should prompt an appointment before the next scheduled service.

**What does prong retipping cost?**
Prong retipping costs vary by bench and by the complexity of the work, but typical retail prices run from twenty to sixty dollars per prong. A four-prong solitaire ring with all four prong tips retipped costs eighty to two hundred and forty dollars, depending on the studio and the metal being worked. This is the most cost-effective maintenance operation in fine jewelry relative to the value it protects.

**Do I need to service pieces I never wear?**
Pieces in storage do not experience the mechanical wear that prompts most service needs, but they should still be inspected periodically. Storage conditions can introduce their own issues — a piece stored in contact with another piece may develop surface scratches; a piece stored in an oxidizing environment may develop tarnish. Every three to five years is an appropriate inspection schedule for unworn pieces.

**What happens to my jewelry while it is at the bench for service?**
A reputable bench should provide a written receipt itemizing every piece left, including a description of each piece and the stated reason it was left. Never leave jewelry with a jeweler who does not issue a written receipt. During service, your pieces are at the bench, in the jeweler's safe overnight, and under the jeweler's professional responsibility. Ask about the studio's insurance coverage for pieces in their care before leaving anything of significant value.
