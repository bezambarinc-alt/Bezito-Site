*Platinum and white gold are different metals with different properties and different lifetime costs — the choice between them deserves more than a visual comparison.*

---

### The Materials
## What Platinum and White Gold Actually Are

Platinum used in fine jewelry is typically 95 percent pure platinum alloyed with 5 percent iridium or ruthenium. The common stamp is PT950 or PLAT. Platinum is a naturally white metal — its white color does not come from any surface treatment and does not change with wear, exposure, or time. At the molecular level, platinum is dense, heavy, and extremely stable. It does not react with most common chemicals, does not oxidize, and does not corrode under normal wear conditions.

White gold is yellow gold alloyed with white metals — typically palladium, nickel, or a combination — to produce an alloy that is less yellow than standard gold. The resulting alloy in 18k white gold (75 percent gold, 25 percent white alloy metals) is not actually white — it is a light grayish-yellow. The white color you see in a white gold ring comes from a surface electroplating of rhodium, a dense, bright-white metal in the platinum group. That rhodium coating is not permanent. It wears at a rate determined by wear frequency, the wearer's skin chemistry, and the thickness of the original plating.

The physical density difference between the two metals is significant and perceptible. Platinum is approximately 60 percent heavier than 18k white gold at equivalent dimensions. A platinum solitaire ring feels meaningfully heavier on the finger than an identical design in white gold. Some wearers prefer this heft; others find it uncomfortable for daily wear. This is a subjective factor with no right answer, but it should be experienced before the purchase decision is finalized.

### The Durability
## How Each Metal Wears and What That Means in Practice

Platinum and white gold do not wear the same way, and understanding the difference prevents unrealistic expectations about the condition of each metal type after years of daily wear.

Platinum is extremely resistant to material loss. A platinum ring worn daily for twenty years will have lost a negligible amount of metal from wear. However, platinum displaces rather than loses material under abrasion — scratches in platinum move metal to the side of the scratch rather than removing it. The result is a surface texture called a patina that develops over time as the polish is disrupted and the surface takes on a lower-gloss, satin-like quality. Some clients find this patina beautiful; others prefer high-polish and return the ring periodically for polishing. Both responses are valid, and polishing platinum to its original state requires professional equipment but is straightforward.

White gold loses material through normal abrasion — gold alloy is softer than platinum and scratches remove actual metal from the surface. The rate of material loss depends on the gold karat and the specific alloy composition, but over decades, high-wear areas of a white gold ring will show measurable metal reduction. In addition to this underlying wear, the rhodium plating coating the white gold wears through to the base alloy, revealing the grayish-yellow tone of the underlying white gold. Replating restores the white color and is necessary every two to five years for a ring worn daily, depending on the wear rate.

### The Cost
## Initial Price, Lifetime Maintenance, and True Total Cost

Platinum costs more than white gold at the time of fabrication. The reasons are the higher cost of the metal itself, the higher fabrication cost (platinum requires different bench tools and higher working temperatures than gold), and the higher per-unit cost of the denser material — a platinum ring of equal dimensions contains more metal by weight than a gold equivalent. Premium for platinum over white gold at comparable design complexity typically runs 25 to 40 percent.

The lifetime cost comparison changes when maintenance is included. White gold requires rhodium replating every two to five years for a ring worn daily. The cost of each replating session varies by studio, but typically runs between $50 and $150 per application. Over twenty years of daily wear, a white gold ring may require four to ten replating sessions, adding $200 to $1,500 to its total lifetime cost. Platinum requires no replating and its only maintenance cost is occasional polishing, which is less expensive than replating.

The break-even calculation depends on individual wear patterns and replating frequency, but for a ring intended for daily wear over decades, the platinum premium at purchase often becomes cost-neutral or advantageous when maintenance costs are fully accounted for. This is a useful calculation to complete before choosing between the two materials.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Can I convert a white gold ring to platinum?**
Not directly — you cannot plate a white gold ring with platinum to achieve the same result as a platinum ring. The metals are fundamentally different, and the appearance and durability characteristics of platinum come from the metal throughout the piece, not from its surface. If you want your existing design in platinum, the ring must be recast or hand-fabricated from platinum. Stones from the existing piece can typically be reused in the new platinum setting.

**Does platinum yellow with age?**
No. Platinum does not contain yellow gold and its white color comes from the metal itself rather than from any surface treatment. Platinum develops a patina from wear that reduces its surface gloss, but the underlying color does not change. The patina can be polished away to restore high-gloss at any time.

**Is platinum hypoallergenic?**
Platinum jewelry is essentially inert and is the recommended choice for wearers with metal sensitivities. The nickel content in some white gold alloys is the most common cause of skin reactions to fine jewelry metals. 18k white gold made with palladium alloy (rather than nickel) is significantly less likely to cause reactions, but platinum's complete absence of nickel and base metals makes it the safer choice for sensitive wearers.

**Can platinum settings be repaired if damaged?**
Yes, platinum is highly repairable. Platinum's malleability allows it to be reformed and welded without significant structural compromise, and the repair does not alter the color of the surrounding metal the way gold repairs can. A cracked or broken platinum shank is a routine bench repair. The repair is often stronger than the original at the repaired point because the welded area is fully consolidated.

**Which metal is better for an engagement ring expected to last a lifetime?**
For a ring intended to be worn daily for fifty or more years, platinum is the superior choice from a purely material standpoint. It does not change color, does not lose significant metal through normal wear, and does not require periodic replating to maintain its appearance. The higher initial cost is offset by lower maintenance expense over the ring's lifetime. For clients who value a lighter feel or a lower initial price point, white gold with an established replating schedule is a reasonable alternative.
