*Emeralds have been oiled, filled, and enhanced for centuries — knowing where your stone sits on that scale determines what you are actually buying.*

---

### The Inclusion Reality
## Why Emeralds Are Almost Never Clean

Every gemstone has inclusions. Emeralds have more than most, and the trade has a specific name for them: the "jardin," French for garden. The internal landscape of fissures, crystals, and growth planes in an emerald is not a defect — it is the fingerprint of the stone. An eye-clean emerald is exceptionally rare in sizes above one carat and commands enormous premiums. Buyers accustomed to diamond grading standards will find themselves either perpetually disappointed or, worse, pushed toward treated stones that appear cleaner than they are.

Eye-visible inclusions in an emerald are acceptable in a way they would not be in a diamond, provided they do not threaten the structural integrity of the stone. Inclusions that reach the surface and compromise durability are a different matter. The key is distinguishing between inclusions that are part of the stone's natural character and fractures that create physical weakness. A good gemologist can identify the difference in minutes.

### Oil, Resin, and the Treatment Scale
## Understanding What Has Been Done to the Stone

Cedar oil has been used to fill surface fractures in emeralds for generations. The practice is so universal that the trade adopted a formal grading system for it. The GIA and Gübelin laboratories grade emerald clarity enhancement on a scale from "none" to "minor," "moderate," "significant," and "highly significant." The difference between "minor" and "significant" can represent a 50 percent price differential on otherwise comparable stones.

Modern treatments go beyond cedar oil. Synthetic resins with a refractive index close to that of emerald are now common, offering better clarity improvement and longer durability than oil. The problem is that resin treatments are not reversible, and they change the character of the stone more fundamentally than traditional oiling. A laboratory report is the only way to know what has been done and to what degree. Buying a significant emerald without a Gübelin or GIA report that addresses enhancement level is a mistake I have seen buyers regret many times.

### Colombian vs. Zambian vs. Brazilian
## Origin and Its Influence on Color

Colombian emeralds from the Muzo and Chivor mines remain the global benchmark. The combination of pure green hue, characteristic warm secondary tone, and the specific fluorescence of Colombian material under UV light gives it a visual quality that buyers return to. The finest Colombian stones — vivid, well-saturated, minimally enhanced — command prices that rival comparable rubies and sapphires.

Zambian emeralds from the Kafubu district have a slightly cooler, more bluish-green tone and often show better clarity than Colombian material. They trade at a discount to Colombian stones of equal apparent quality, which makes them excellent value for buyers who prefer the cooler green. Brazilian emeralds vary widely; Belmont material has been producing stones with strong color and decent clarity, but the origin carries less cache in the collector market.

### Setting Considerations
## Protecting an Emerald in Jewelry

Emeralds are harder than most people expect — 7.5 to 8 on the Mohs scale — but they fracture more easily than sapphire or diamond because of their inclusion content. A bezel setting or a low-profile prong configuration reduces the risk of chipping at the girdle, and it is worth the design constraint. Emeralds should not be in settings that expose the stone to significant lateral force.

The stepped rectangular cut known as the emerald cut was not named after the stone by accident — it was developed specifically for emerald because it minimizes the cutting pressure that would otherwise cause fracturing. Oval and pear shapes are also well-suited to emerald rough. Round emeralds exist but require more rough per finished carat and are accordingly more expensive.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How important is the "none" or "minor" oil treatment grade?**
Extremely important. A well-colored emerald with "none" or "minor" enhancement is worth substantially more than a stone with "significant" or "highly significant" treatment — even if the two stones look similar to an untrained eye. The lab report is not a formality; it is the price anchor.

**Can I clean an emerald in an ultrasonic cleaner?**
No. Ultrasonic cleaners will drive out cedar oil, displace resin, and can cause fractures to propagate in heavily included stones. Warm water with a drop of mild soap and a soft brush is all you should use. Recheck with your jeweler periodically to see if re-oiling is advisable.

**What size emerald makes a meaningful center stone for a ring?**
The practical minimum for visual impact in a center stone position is about 0.75 carats. Most of our clients working in the emerald category are looking at stones between 1.5 and 4 carats. Above 3 carats, finding strong color with acceptable inclusions and minimal enhancement becomes considerably harder.

**Is Colombian origin worth the premium?**
For a collector buying exceptional material, yes. For most jewelry buyers, a well-colored Zambian emerald with minor enhancement and a good laboratory report will give more visual satisfaction per dollar. Colombian origin matters most at the high end where the nuances of tone and fluorescence become the decisive factors.

**What metal complements an emerald best?**
Yellow gold enhances the warm undertones in Colombian material and was the historic setting choice for the great Colombian stones of the Spanish colonial period. Platinum and white gold create stronger color contrast and make the green appear cooler and more vivid. Both are legitimate — the choice depends on personal preference and whether other stones in the piece lean warm or cool.
