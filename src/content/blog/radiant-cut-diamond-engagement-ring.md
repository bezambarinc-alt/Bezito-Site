*The radiant cut packs more scintillation per square millimeter than almost any other fancy shape — and understanding why changes how you shop for one.*

---

### The Architecture
## A Cut Engineered for Maximum Light Return

The radiant cut was introduced in 1977 by diamond cutter Henry Grossbard, who wanted to bring the pavilion complexity of a round brilliant to a rectangular outline. He succeeded. Beneath the flat table of every radiant lies a fully faceted pavilion — 70 facets total — that breaks incoming light into the fine, crushed-ice sparkle the cut is famous for. No other rectangular shape does this. The emerald cut reflects broad, hall-of-mirrors flashes. The princess cut produces bold geometric bursts. The radiant produces something closer to continuous fire, the kind that catches the eye from across a room.

That distinction matters at the counter. Buyers who choose a radiant are usually choosing sensation over statement. They want a ring that moves, that changes in candlelight, that refuses to look the same twice. If you want a stone that reads as a quiet architectural object, the emerald cut is your answer. If you want a stone that performs, the radiant is yours.

### Shape Ratio
## Length-to-Width Determines Everything About Silhouette

Radiants are cut in a range of proportions, and the ratio between length and width defines the character of the stone on the hand. A ratio of 1.00 to 1.05 produces a square radiant, sometimes called a square modified brilliant, which sits compact and bold on shorter fingers. A ratio of 1.20 to 1.35 produces the classic elongated radiant — the shape that photographs well, that lengthens the finger visually, and that most buyers are picturing when they say they want a radiant.

Ratios above 1.40 exist but require careful evaluation. A very elongated radiant concentrates more of its weight toward the ends, which can expose the corners to chip risk if the setting does not protect them. This is not a reason to avoid elongated stones — it is a reason to work with a jeweler who builds settings with corner protection as a structural priority, not an afterthought.

Color grading behaves differently in a radiant than in a round brilliant. The crushed-ice faceting pattern retains warmth toward the center of the stone. A stone graded G in a round may read as white; the same G in a large radiant may show a faint cream tone near the table. For stones above two carats, moving to F or E in color repays the investment visibly.

### Setting the Stone
## The Mount Either Elevates the Cut or Fights It

A radiant cut at two carats and above carries enough visual weight to anchor almost any setting style, which is both its strength and its risk. The stone does not need ornamentation to hold attention. Overbuilt settings — crowded pavé shoulders, competing halo proportions, asymmetric stacking that draws the eye away from center — reduce the radiant to one element among many. The stone came to the ring to be the point. Let it.

The settings that work best are those designed with discipline. A four-prong solitaire with sharp, angular prong tips echoes the geometry of the cut and stays out of the way. A six-prong cathedral mount elevates the stone and allows light to enter the pavilion from the side, increasing face-up brightness. A bezel is a legitimate choice for buyers who want security and a modern silhouette, though a full bezel blocks lateral light entry and slightly reduces scintillation — a trade-off worth understanding before committing.

### Quality Priorities
## Where to Spend and Where to Save

Cut quality in a radiant is reported by grading laboratories as a range of grades rather than a single grade, because the GIA (Gemological Institute of America) does not assign overall cut grades to fancy shapes as it does to round brilliants. This places the evaluation burden on the jeweler and the buyer. The metrics that matter most are depth percentage, table percentage, and crown angle — and they need to be read together, not in isolation.

A well-cut radiant typically falls between 61 and 67 percent depth. Above 70 percent, the stone looks smaller face-up than its carat weight suggests. Below 59 percent, the pavilion loses the structural depth needed to return light properly and the stone goes dark in the center. Table percentages between 61 and 69 work across most cutting styles. Outside that range, light return becomes unpredictable.

Clarity grades of VS2 (very slightly included, second degree) and above are generally eye-clean in radiants. The crushed-ice faceting pattern conceals inclusions more effectively than step cuts, which means an SI1 (slightly included, first degree) stone can often be worn with no visible flaws — but only after physical inspection, not on the basis of a certificate alone. Every stone is different. Look at the stone.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What is the difference between a radiant cut and a princess cut?**
Both are square or rectangular diamonds with high facet counts, but they are structurally distinct. A princess cut has pointed corners and a faceting pattern that produces bold, geometric flashes of light. A radiant cut has cropped corners and a fully faceted pavilion that generates finer, more continuous sparkle. The radiant also comes in a wider range of length-to-width ratios than the princess, giving buyers more silhouette options.

**Do radiant cut diamonds show color more than rounds?**
In larger sizes, yes. The crushed-ice faceting of a radiant can concentrate warmth toward the center of the stone in ways that a round brilliant's faceting disperses. For stones above 1.5 carats, most buyers are better served by a color grade of F or above to ensure the face-up appearance reads as white. Below 1.5 carats, a G or H typically looks clean in white metal.

**Are radiant cut diamonds more affordable than round brilliants?**
Carat for carat, radiant cuts are priced meaningfully lower than round brilliants of equivalent quality. The round brilliant commands a premium because the cutting process wastes more of the rough crystal and because demand for rounds remains the highest of any shape. A buyer who moves from a 1.50-carat round to a 1.50-carat radiant of the same color and clarity grade can often redirect the savings into a better stone or a more refined setting.

**What metal works best with a radiant cut diamond?**
Platinum and white gold both complement the radiant's geometric outline and allow the stone's color to read neutrally. Yellow gold works well with lower color grades — the warm metal absorbs any residual warmth in the stone and makes it disappear. Rose gold produces a warmer, more romantic tone that suits buyers who want softness in a cut that is inherently structural. The choice is aesthetic, not technical.

**How should radiant cut corners be protected in a setting?**
The cropped corners of a radiant cut are less vulnerable than the sharp points of a princess cut, but they still benefit from corner prongs or bezel coverage at the tips. A well-built four-prong or six-prong setting places prongs at the corners rather than along the sides, which protects the most exposed part of the stone without blocking light entry. Ask your jeweler to show you how the prongs sit before the stone is set.
