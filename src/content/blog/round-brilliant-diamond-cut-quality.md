*The round brilliant is the most studied object in the history of gemology, and after forty years of cutting and setting diamonds, I will tell you plainly: cut is the only variable that separates a stone that commands a room from one that simply sits there.*

---

### What Cut Actually
## Controls Inside a Diamond

A diamond does not generate light. It redirects it. The round brilliant's 57 or 58 facets — arranged in a precise geometry across the crown, girdle, and pavilion — are engineered to capture rays entering from above, bounce them between the pavilion facets, and return them through the table directly to the observer's eye. When that geometry is correct, you get three things: brilliance (the white light), fire (the spectral color flares), and scintillation (the on-off flash as the stone or the viewer moves).

When the geometry is off, even slightly, light escapes through the pavilion floor or leaks out the sides. The stone goes dark in the center — what cutters call a "fish-eye" or "nail head" — and no amount of color or clarity grade will rescue it. You can own a D Flawless diamond that looks like a piece of glass. I have seen it happen. The client paid a premium for two grades that the cut then erased.

### The Numbers That
## Define an Excellent Cut

The Gemological Institute of America (GIA) grades cut on round brilliants only — a decision made after decades of modeling light behavior. Their Excellent tier has specific proportional corridors: table percentage between 54 and 57 percent, total depth between 59 and 62.5 percent, crown angle between 34 and 35 degrees, pavilion angle between 40.6 and 41 degrees. The crown and pavilion angles are the most critical pairing. A crown of 34.5 degrees matched with a pavilion of 40.8 degrees produces a stone that exhibits both high brilliance and strong fire simultaneously.

The AGS (American Gem Society) laboratories use a ray-tracing model called the Performance-based system and issue grades of 0 through 10, where 0 is their equivalent of Excellent. AGS 0 and GIA Excellent overlap substantially, but AGS often applies stricter tolerances on girdle thickness and cutlet size. Either grade, from either lab, on a well-proportioned stone, will perform.

What neither grade captures fully is the human eye's response to a specific stone under specific light. This is why I have always insisted that clients look at finalists side by side, face up, under a single overhead source. The numbers tell you the stone has the right architecture. The eye tells you which one speaks.

### Polish, Symmetry, and
## The Details That Amplify Performance

GIA and AGS both issue separate grades for polish and symmetry, and both should be Excellent or Very Good on any stone you are seriously considering. Polish describes the quality of each facet surface — tiny polishing lines visible only under magnification can scatter light at the surface before it even enters the stone. Symmetry describes how accurately each of the 57 facets mirrors its opposite: a heart-and-arrows pattern, visible through a special viewer, indicates that the cutter achieved near-perfect rotational symmetry. This is not cosmetic. Asymmetrical facets create unequal light path lengths and produce a stone that flashes unevenly.

The girdle — the thin band separating crown from pavilion — should be described as thin to slightly thick on the grading report. An extremely thin girdle is prone to chipping during setting. An extremely thick one adds weight without adding diameter, meaning you pay for more carats than you see from above.

### The Price Penalty for
## Choosing Cut Over Carat

Here is the honest math. A round brilliant cut to GIA Excellent with Excellent polish and symmetry will cost you eight to fifteen percent more than the same carat weight cut to Very Good or Good. That premium is one of the most rational spending decisions in the diamond market, because the Excellent stone will face-up larger, return more light, and hold its desirability in the secondary market far better than its underperforming sibling.

I have watched buyers go one-quarter carat heavier to save money, end up with a Good-cut stone, and then wonder six months later why the ring does not have the presence they expected. Cut is not a feature you can add later. It is set at the wheel, and it is set forever.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does a higher cut grade always mean more brilliance?**

Within the Excellent tier, yes — but "more" is a relative term. The difference between a middling Excellent and a top-performing Excellent may be invisible to most observers under normal lighting. What the grade guarantees is that the stone meets the minimum performance threshold for that tier; exceptional stones within the tier have proportions that cluster around specific crown-and-pavilion angle combinations that cutters and gemologists track separately.

**Is GIA Excellent the same thing as AGS 0?**

They are functionally equivalent for most buyers, but the grading systems use different methodologies. GIA uses a proportions-based approach with empirically defined corridors; AGS uses a light-performance model that simulates ray tracing. An AGS 0 stone and a GIA Excellent stone will both perform beautifully. The difference becomes relevant only in edge cases where a stone sits at the boundary of proportional acceptability.

**Can I get an Excellent cut in a size below half a carat?**

Yes, though the tolerances become harder to achieve at very small sizes. GIA grades cut on rounds as small as 0.15 carats. Below that threshold, grading is not offered, but a skilled cutter working to ideal proportions can still produce a high-performing stone. Ask to see the stone face-up alongside a reference Excellent stone at the same size.

**Why do some Excellent-graded diamonds look better than others?**

Because the Excellent grade is a range, not a single point. Stones at the center of the range — with crown angles near 34.5 degrees and pavilion angles near 40.8 degrees — tend to show the best balance of brilliance and fire. Stones at the edges of the grade may pass the threshold but sacrifice one optical property for another. A reputable dealer will know where in the grade a specific stone falls.

**Does cut grade affect the value at resale?**

Meaningfully, yes. GIA Excellent stones are the preferred inventory of estate dealers, auction houses, and online secondary markets. A stone with a Fair or Poor cut grade can become functionally unsaleable at a fair price, even if the color and clarity grades are excellent, because sophisticated buyers know they would be paying for weight they cannot see.
