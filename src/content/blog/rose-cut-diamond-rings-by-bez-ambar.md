*The rose cut was designed for candlelight in the sixteenth century. Its domed profile and large flat facets captured ambient light in a way the modern brilliant cut was not designed for.*

---

The rose cut emerged from Flemish and Dutch cutting workshops in the early to mid-1500s. The design logic was practical: a flat base, a domed top, and a relatively small number of triangular facets, typically 24, arranged to catch and reflect available light. In an era when the primary artificial light source was an open flame, the rose cut performed well because it spread light broadly across its surface rather than concentrating it into directed flashes.

The modern round brilliant, developed in the early twentieth century, was optimized for electric light: controlled and bright, making it possible to design for precisely directed reflection and refraction. The brilliant cut produces more fire and scintillation than a rose cut under equivalent conditions. But it produces a different kind of light, and different is not always better.

### The Physical
## Difference

A standard rose cut has a flat base and a rounded dome with 24 triangular facets meeting at an apex. The height of the dome varies: a high-dome rose cut has more depth and pronounced curvature; a low-dome or flat rose cut sits almost flush with the setting.

Because the base is flat, a rose cut diamond does not require the pointed pavilion of a brilliant-cut stone. This means it can be set directly against a surface, or held in a flat bezel, in ways a brilliant cut cannot accommodate. Tablet settings, where the stone rests flat against a gold base, are a characteristic rose cut approach.

The flat base also means that the weight of a rose cut concentrates in the dome rather than splitting between crown and pavilion. For a given carat weight, a rose cut faces up significantly larger than a brilliant cut of the same weight: more of the mass spreads horizontally across the table. A 1.00-carat rose cut can face up 10 to 20 percent larger than a 1.00-carat round brilliant.

### The Light
## It Produces

Under soft ambient light, a rose cut produces a gentle, distributed glow. The large facets spread light broadly across the dome rather than concentrating it into the precise points of brilliance characteristic of modern cuts. The effect is warm and even, sometimes described as romantic, though that word is an accurate description of an optical difference rather than a sentimental claim.

Under bright direct light, a rose cut can appear dimmer than a round brilliant of comparable quality because it lacks the deep pavilion that sends white light back through the table in concentrated flashes. This is physics: a different cut, optimized for different conditions.

Rose cuts perform beautifully in the lighting conditions most people actually live in: indoor light with natural window light, candlelight, soft evening light. They look the way they were designed to look in everyday environments.

### Setting
## Considerations

The flat base of a rose cut opens up setting options that do not work with brilliant cuts. A bezel setting that holds the dome while the flat base rests against the bezel floor is clean and secure. A collet setting, where the stone sits in a low cup of metal, is another traditional approach.

The low profile of a flat rose cut sits closer to the hand than a prong-set brilliant, which many people find more comfortable for daily wear. It is less likely to catch on fabrics and has less leverage for impact because it projects less from the finger.

Bez Ambar designs settings for rose cut diamonds that take advantage of the stone's specific geometry: the wide face, the domed profile, the flat base. A setting designed for a rose cut makes better use of the stone than a standard brilliant-cut mounting with a flat-bottom modification.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does a rose cut diamond sparkle?**
Not in the way a modern brilliant cut does. A rose cut produces a softer, more distributed glow rather than precise points of fire and brilliance. Under candlelight and soft indoor light, many people find rose cuts as beautiful as brilliant cuts. Under very bright direct light, a rose cut appears quieter than a brilliant cut. This is a physical difference, not a quality issue.

**Does a rose cut face up larger than a round brilliant?**
Yes, typically. Because all the weight sits in the dome rather than split between crown and pavilion, a rose cut of a given carat weight spreads more surface area than a brilliant cut of the same weight. A 1.00-carat rose cut often appears comparable to a 1.15 to 1.20-carat round brilliant when viewed face-up on the hand.

**What era are rose cut diamonds from?**
Rose cut diamonds were the dominant cut from the 1500s through the early 1900s, when the modern round brilliant replaced them for most applications. Much antique and vintage jewelry from the Georgian, Victorian, and Edwardian periods features rose cuts. Contemporary jewelers work with both antique rose cuts sourced from old jewelry and newly cut rose cuts produced today.

**Are rose cut diamonds less valuable than round brilliants?**
Per carat, rose cut diamonds are typically less expensive than comparable round brilliants because the market for them is smaller and because modern cutting standards favor brilliance. For buyers who prefer the aesthetic, this represents value: a larger-appearing stone for less money.

**What setting works best for a rose cut diamond?**
Bezel and collet settings are traditional and appropriate because the flat base rests securely against the setting floor. Low-prong settings also work. High-prong settings designed for pavilioned brilliant cuts are not the right approach for a rose cut and can result in proportions that look off.
