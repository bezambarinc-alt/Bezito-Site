*Shape answers the question "what does the diamond look like?" Cut answers the question "how well does the diamond work?" A round brilliant with a poor cut is a beautiful shape with mediocre performance.*

---

### Shape: The Outline
## What Shape Refers to and the Available Options

Shape is the silhouette of the diamond as seen from above — the two-dimensional outline of the polished stone. The major shape categories are round, oval, pear, marquise (a pointed oval), heart, cushion, radiant, princess, emerald, and asscher. Each shape is a different starting decision about how the diamond's mass will be distributed across the face-up surface and how the stone will read on the hand.

Round brilliants are the most common shape by volume because they combine maximum cutting efficiency in terms of light return with the most universally understood engagement ring visual language. Every other shape is classified as a "fancy shape" in the trade, regardless of how conventional or unconventional it appears. A princess cut is a fancy shape; a pear is a fancy shape; an emerald cut is a fancy shape.

Shape selection is fundamentally a matter of personal aesthetic and practical fit. Some shapes elongate the finger visually — ovals, pears, and marquise cuts extend along the finger's length and make the hand appear longer and more slender. Square and rectangular shapes — princess, radiant, asscher, emerald — feel more architectural and geometric. Round is the most neutral option and reads as classically bridal across cultural contexts.

Shape choice also affects carat efficiency — how large a given weight appears on the hand. The oval is typically the most efficient fancy shape for face-up size per carat weight, because its elongated outline creates the impression of a larger stone than a round of the same weight. The round brilliant, by contrast, concentrates all its mass in a compact circular footprint and tends to appear slightly smaller per carat than elongated shapes.

### Cut: The Quality of Execution
## What Cut Grading Measures and Why It Matters

Cut refers to how precisely and skillfully the shape was executed — specifically, how the facet geometry, proportions, symmetry, and polish of the finished stone produce optical performance. Two round brilliants of identical shape, color, and clarity grades can differ dramatically in cut quality, and that difference determines whether the stone appears bright, brilliant, and alive or dull, flat, and lifeless.

GIA's (Gemological Institute of America) cut grading for round brilliant diamonds is the industry standard evaluation. It assesses seven components: brightness (the total light reflected from the stone), fire (the dispersion of light into its spectral colors), scintillation (the pattern of light and dark areas during movement), weight ratio (the relationship between the stone's weight and its apparent size), durability (the girdle thickness and culet size that affect resistance to damage), polish (the quality of the finished facet surfaces), and symmetry (the precision of facet alignment and proportion matching). The overall Cut grade — Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, or Poor — summarizes the evaluation across all these components.

For fancy shapes, GIA does not issue an overall Cut grade. This is the critical gap that creates risk for fancy-shape buyers who assume shape and cut are the same question. An oval diamond has no GIA Cut grade — it has Polish and Symmetry grades, which are necessary but not sufficient. Two GIA-certified ovals with identical Excellent Polish and Symmetry grades can still differ substantially in light performance, bow-tie effect severity, and overall visual quality. Evaluating fancy shape cut requires looking at the stone, not just the certificate.

### The Bow-Tie and Other Shape-Specific Cut Issues
## What to Watch for in Fancy Shapes

Several fancy shapes are prone to specific optical defects that result from poor cutting proportions. The bow-tie is the most common: a dark, butterfly-shaped shadow visible in the center of oval, pear, marquise, and sometimes elongated cushion diamonds. All diamonds with elongated outlines exhibit some degree of bow-tie because the shape's geometry creates a zone in the center where light cannot return efficiently from the pavilion. The severity ranges from barely perceptible to dominant — a strong bow-tie that occupies most of the stone's center is a significant visual defect that reduces the stone's appeal and value.

The bow-tie is not listed on GIA certificates. It can only be evaluated by examining the stone in person or through high-quality video. A well-cut oval with a minor bow-tie that disappears as the stone moves is normal and acceptable. An oval where a dark tie is prominent even when the stone is still is a cutting deficiency that should be reflected in a significant price reduction.

Step-cut shapes — emerald and asscher — do not produce bow-ties but have their own cutting sensitivity. Because their parallel facets act like windows into the stone rather than reflectors, poorly proportioned step cuts show their pavilion directly and any asymmetry in pavilion depth becomes visible as uneven darkening across the stone's face. The "Hall of Mirrors" effect in a well-cut emerald — the infinite-regress pattern of reflections down through the stone — is the result of precise pavilion parallelism. A poorly cut emerald loses this effect and appears flat.

### Practical Buying Guidance
## Making the Shape-Cut Decision Correctly

Shape should be chosen first, based on the wearer's aesthetic preferences and the practical characteristics of each outline — its finger appearance, its setting options, its visual vocabulary. This is a personal decision that has no objectively correct answer.

Cut should then be maximized within the budget allocated to the chosen shape. For round brilliants, GIA Excellent or AGS (American Gem Society) Ideal 0 grades reliably identify the top tier of cutting performance. For fancy shapes, that assurance requires individual stone evaluation. The minimum acceptable standard for any fancy shape is GIA Excellent Polish and Symmetry grades, but those grades are necessary without being sufficient.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is "princess cut" a cut grade or a shape?**
Princess is a shape — a square or near-square outline with brilliant-style faceting. The cut quality of a princess-cut diamond is evaluated separately by the quality of its proportions and symmetry, not by the shape designation itself. Two princess-cut diamonds with the same GIA color and clarity grade can be cut beautifully or poorly; the shape name tells you nothing about that.

**Why doesn't GIA give cut grades to fancy shapes?**
GIA's round brilliant cut grade is based on decades of optical modeling research that established specific proportion ranges that reliably produce optimal performance for that symmetrical shape. Fancy shapes vary too widely in their length-to-width ratios and silhouette nuances to be captured by a single set of ideal proportions. GIA has indicated interest in extending cut grading to oval shapes, but as of the current date, the overall cut grade applies only to round brilliants.

**Does shape affect diamond price per carat?**
Yes, significantly. Round brilliants typically trade at a 20 to 40 percent premium over equivalent fancy shapes because they require more rough diamond to cut — more carat weight is lost in cutting a round than an oval or pear from the same rough. Among fancy shapes, marquise and pear cuts tend to be slightly less expensive per carat than ovals because demand is lower. Emerald and asscher cuts are typically priced between rounds and ovals.

**Which shape is best for a small diamond?**
Round brilliants are forgiving of smaller sizes because their cutting pattern maximizes light return and scintillation per unit of surface area. Elongated shapes like ovals and marquise appear larger than rounds of the same weight, which can be useful at smaller sizes. Step cuts — emerald and asscher — tend to look better in larger sizes, where their open tables have enough surface area to show the depth and reflections that are their defining character.

**Can the same diamond shape look different in different settings?**
Substantially so. An oval diamond in a simple solitaire with a thin band reads as modern and minimalist. The same oval diamond in a halo setting with a pavé band reads as elaborate and glamorous. Shape interacts strongly with setting design, and the same shape can produce very different visual outcomes depending on the mount. The best approach is to evaluate the complete ring — stone and setting together — rather than assessing the shape in isolation.
