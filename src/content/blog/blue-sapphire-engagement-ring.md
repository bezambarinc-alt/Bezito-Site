*Blue sapphires have been used in royal engagement rings for centuries. What makes them genuinely suitable for daily wear, and what to know before buying one, is a different conversation from the romance.*

---

When Prince Charles gave Princess Diana an 18-carat oval sapphire ring in 1981, he was working within a tradition that stretched back centuries. Sapphires appeared in medieval clergy rings, in Renaissance court jewelry, in Victorian betrothal rings alongside diamonds. The stone's association with fidelity and sincerity predates the diamond engagement ring tradition by several hundred years.

Prince William gave Diana's ring to Kate Middleton in 2010, and the global interest in sapphire engagement rings that followed was substantial. But the reasons to consider a sapphire go beyond royal association, and the reasons to be cautious are also worth knowing.

### Origin
## and Color

Blue sapphires come from several primary sources, each with its own color character and market reputation.

Kashmir sapphires, mined from a high-altitude deposit in the Indian Himalaya, produce the most prized stones: a deep, velvety blue with a quality often described as "sleepy." The Kashmir deposit is essentially exhausted, making these stones extremely rare and valuable.

Ceylon sapphires (from Sri Lanka) produce a brighter, more electric blue. They are the most commercially significant source of fine blue sapphires and range from light cornflower blue to deep royal blue.

Burmese sapphires from the Mogok Valley in Myanmar historically produced stones with vivid, saturated blue color. Geopolitical complications affect their availability.

Montana sapphires, mined in the United States, produce distinctive steel-blue to blue-green stones unlike any other source. They are increasingly sought by buyers who want American origin stones.

The color you are looking for in a blue sapphire is saturation without darkness. A stone that is too light reads as pale and washed-out. A stone that is too dark absorbs light and looks nearly black in dim conditions. The sweet spot is a medium to medium-dark blue with good saturation and a blue that stays visible in varied lighting.

### Hardness
## and Wear

Sapphire is corundum, ranking 9 on the Mohs scale of hardness. Diamond is 10. This is not a small distinction: the Mohs scale is logarithmic, meaning diamond is significantly harder than sapphire. But sapphire is still among the hardest materials you will encounter in daily life. For an engagement ring worn every day for decades, sapphire is genuinely appropriate. The surface will develop minor scratches over time, but so do diamonds; they are simply less visible because diamond scratches require a diamond to inflict them.

The comparison that matters for daily wear is not sapphire versus diamond but sapphire versus everything else your ring will encounter: countertops, keys, metal surfaces. At 9 on the Mohs scale, sapphire handles all of these without issue.

### Treatment
## Disclosure

Most blue sapphires on the market have been heat-treated to improve color and clarity. This is an industry-standard practice that has been performed for decades and is accepted by all major gemological laboratories, including GIA. Heat treatment is not a negative; it is a standard refinement process analogous to cutting and polishing.

What is not acceptable: beryllium diffusion treatment, where beryllium is diffused into the stone at high temperature to alter its color dramatically. This is a deceptive treatment that should be disclosed and is not considered equivalent to heat treatment. A GIA or AGL certificate will identify whether a sapphire has been heat-treated or subjected to other treatments.

For a stone of significant value, always require gemological documentation.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is a blue sapphire engagement ring durable enough for daily wear?**
Yes. At 9 on the Mohs scale, sapphire is the second hardest gemstone used in jewelry. It handles daily wear better than most other gemstones. Over many years, minor surface scratches will accumulate, but this is true of all gemstones except diamond. The ring can be polished periodically to restore its surface.

**What color of blue sapphire is most valuable?**
The most prized color is a vivid, saturated medium-to-medium-dark blue with no grey or green modifier, often described as "cornflower blue" or "royal blue." Kashmir sapphires are the benchmark for the finest blue color. In practical terms, look for a stone where the blue remains visible and saturated in both bright light and dim conditions.

**Should I choose a heated or unheated sapphire?**
Most sapphires on the market are heat-treated, which is acceptable and standard. Untreated (unheated) sapphires of fine color command a significant premium because they are rarer, and their natural color is considered more valuable by collectors. For most buyers, a fine heat-treated sapphire is an excellent choice; the premium for unheated is worthwhile only if natural origin is important to you.

**How does a sapphire engagement ring hold its value?**
The resale market for sapphires is less liquid than for diamonds. Diamond prices are relatively standardized; sapphire prices vary significantly by origin, treatment, and specific color characteristics. A fine, well-documented sapphire from a prestigious origin holds value reasonably well, but it requires a more specialized buyer than a diamond would.
