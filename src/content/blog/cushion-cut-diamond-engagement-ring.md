### The Shape
## A Cut That Predates the Modern Diamond Industry

The cushion cut is the oldest surviving brilliant-style diamond shape. Its rounded corners and pillow-like outline trace back to the early 1800s, when cutters worked by hand and maximized carat weight over geometric precision. What they produced — the old mine cut — was romantic in a way that defies the mechanical perfection of the modern round brilliant. The cushion cut is that shape's direct descendant, refined over generations but never stripped of its essential warmth.

The name describes what you see: a square or rectangular stone with softened corners that give it the look of a cushion, or a pillow. Beneath that gentle outline lives a facet structure designed to pool light in the center of the stone and release it in slow, rolling flashes. It is not the same performance as a round brilliant, which disperses light rapidly and uniformly. The cushion moves differently — deeper, warmer, with a quality that antique collectors have always called fire.

That fire comes from the larger facets that define the cushion's original architecture. Fewer facets, each one larger, means each reflection is distinct. Sunlight hits a cushion-cut diamond and separates into color the way a prism does — fans of spectral light that sweep across the table as the stone turns. This is the property that has kept the cushion in production for over two hundred years while dozens of other shapes have come and gone.

### What Sets It Apart
## Why Cushion Cuts Carry Light Differently Than Any Other Shape

Every diamond shape is a theory about how light should behave inside a stone. The round brilliant's theory is efficiency: maximum return of white light through mathematically optimized angles. The princess cut's theory is modernity — clean corners, tight geometry, high contrast. The cushion's theory is character. It accepts that some light will scatter, that some color will bleed into the room, and it treats that scattering as the point rather than the problem.

Modern cutters have developed two distinct cushion families. The first — sometimes called the chunky cushion — retains the large facets of the old mine cut and produces the classic, romantic fire. The second, often called the crushed ice cushion, adds additional facets that create a sparkling, granular pattern closer to what you see in a radiant cut. Both are legitimate. They suit different people and different settings. A client who wants warmth and history should choose the chunky variety. A client who wants brilliance closer to a round should consider the crushed ice.

### Choosing Well
## The Quality Factors That Define a Cushion Diamond

Cut quality is the most important variable in a cushion, and it is the least standardized. The Gemological Institute of America (GIA) does not issue an overall cut grade for cushion-cut diamonds the way it does for round brilliants. That means buyers cannot rely on a single letter to evaluate cutting quality. You have to look at the stone. Depth percentage, table percentage, and the symmetry of the facet pattern all matter, but none of them tells you as much as placing the diamond in good light and watching it move.

Clarity is more forgiving in cushions than in step cuts like the emerald. The cushion's broken, active facet pattern conceals inclusions that would be conspicuous in a flat, mirror-like emerald cut. Most buyers can comfortably consider VS2 or SI1 stones without visible inclusions. Color is less forgiving. The cushion's deep pavilion retains body color more than shallower shapes, which means a J-color cushion will read more yellow than a J-color round of the same weight. For platinum and white gold settings, stay at H or better. For yellow gold, a warmer stone often reads as intentional and beautiful.

### Setting the Stone
## How the Right Mounting Amplifies a Cushion Diamond's Character

The cushion cut is one of the most setting-adaptable shapes in fine jewelry. Its soft corners eliminate the chipping risk that makes princess cuts vulnerable along their edges. It sits low and stable in solitaire settings, and its symmetry is forgiving enough to accommodate both vintage-style prong configurations and contemporary bezel work.

Four-prong solitaires let the stone breathe and keep the focus entirely on the diamond. Halo settings amplify the cushion's apparent size and reinforce its romantic silhouette — the round halo stones echo the rounded corners of the center diamond in a way that feels deliberate rather than borrowed. Pavé bands add brilliance at the perimeter without competing with the center stone's distinctive fire. The cushion adapts to all of them because its geometry is honest: it has no sharp angles to fight with a setting's architecture.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What is a cushion cut diamond?**
A cushion cut diamond is a square or rectangular brilliant-cut stone with rounded corners that give it a soft, pillow-like outline. The shape dates to the early 1800s and is the direct ancestor of the old mine cut used throughout the Georgian and Victorian eras. Its defining characteristic is a larger-faceted structure that produces distinctive fire — the dispersion of white light into spectral color — rather than the rapid, uniform brilliance of a modern round.

**Is a cushion cut a good choice for an engagement ring?**
It is one of the best choices, and has been for over two centuries. The cushion's soft corners make it durable in a prong setting, its warmth suits a wide range of metal colors, and its romantic character ages well. Trends in diamond cuts come and go; the cushion has never left the market because it appeals to something more fundamental than fashion.

**What is the difference between a chunky cushion and a crushed ice cushion?**
The chunky cushion retains the larger facets of the old mine cut and produces broad flashes of fire and color. The crushed ice cushion has a denser, finer facet pattern that creates a sparkling, granular brilliance closer to what you see in a radiant cut. Both are valid interpretations of the shape. The chunky variety reads as warmer and more antique; the crushed ice reads as more contemporary and high-brilliance.

**How do I evaluate a cushion cut diamond without a GIA cut grade?**
GIA does not issue an overall cut grade for cushion-cut diamonds, so you have to evaluate the stone directly. Place it face-up under a single light source and tilt it slowly. A well-cut cushion will show even, balanced fire across the table without a dark extinction zone in the center. Check the depth percentage — stones cut too deep lose light through the pavilion, and stones cut too shallow spread light unevenly. There is no substitute for seeing the stone in person.

**What color grade should I look for in a cushion cut diamond?**
Because the cushion's deep pavilion retains body color, the shape shows color more than shallower cuts. For a platinum or white gold setting, H color or better keeps the stone looking white face-up. For yellow gold, a warmer stone in the I to J range often reads as complementary to the metal rather than as a deficiency. The choice depends on the setting and on what your eye finds pleasing — not on a single number on a certificate.
