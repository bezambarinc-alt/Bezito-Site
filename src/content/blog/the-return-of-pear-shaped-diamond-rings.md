*The pear shape has been in and out of fashion since Lodewyk van Bercken developed the first complex faceted cuts in 1475. Its current moment is earned rather than accidental.*

---

In 1475, a Flemish cutter named Lodewyk van Bercken developed a polishing wheel, the scaif, that allowed diamond facets to be cut and polished against each other rather than ground on abrasive surfaces. This made complex, asymmetric cuts possible for the first time. The pear shape, combining the rounded base of a round brilliant with a pointed apex, was among the earliest forms to emerge from this technology.

Five and a half centuries later, the pear is experiencing its strongest moment in decades. Understanding why requires understanding what makes it distinctive, and why those qualities resonate right now.

### The Shape
## and Its Properties

A pear brilliant, also called a teardrop cut, has a curved bottom and a tapered point at the top. It combines the faceting pattern of a round brilliant with the elongated geometry of a marquise cut. The result is a stone that has strong light return while also creating a lengthening effect on the finger.

The length-to-width ratio of a pear typically runs between 1.45 and 1.75, with most buyers preferring the range between 1.50 and 1.65. Narrower pears read more like an elongated marquise; wider pears approach the proportions of a rounded square. The ideal ratio is personal, but the middle of that range is the most versatile.

The elongation is the pear's most practical advantage. On the hand, it creates the illusion of a longer, more slender finger, which is why the shape suits a wide range of hand types. Where a round brilliant reads as a centered point of light, a pear creates a line of light across the finger: a different kind of presence.

### The Bow-Tie
## Problem

Most elongated fancy shapes, including ovals, marquise cuts, and pears, can exhibit a bow-tie effect: a dark, shadow-like region running across the width of the stone, roughly where a bow tie would sit on a person. This is caused by light escaping through the pavilion facets rather than reflecting back through the table.

All bow ties exist on a spectrum from barely perceptible to prominently distracting. A faint bow tie adds character; a strong bow tie dominates the stone and reduces its face-up appearance of brightness.

The bow-tie effect cannot be determined from a diamond certificate. GIA and other laboratories do not grade for it. The only way to evaluate a pear for bow-tie is to view it in person, ideally under multiple light conditions. A stone that looks fine under bright overhead lighting may show a more pronounced bow tie in softer ambient light.

When buying a pear, this assessment is not optional. A stone that performs well across lighting conditions is worth paying a premium for over one that photographs well and looks dim on the hand.

### Orientation
## and Setting

The pear is set with either the point toward the fingertip or the point toward the hand. There is no correct answer here: both orientations appear in fine jewelry throughout history and in contemporary design. The point-toward-fingertip orientation is more traditional and reads as more formal. The point-toward-hand orientation is more modern and is increasingly common.

The setting needs to address the point specifically. A pointed apex has only one facet at the tip, making it the most vulnerable part of the stone to chipping. A V-prong that wraps around and protects the tip is standard practice for pear settings. This is not optional: an exposed pear point chips under ordinary daily wear.

The setting also determines how the stone sits on the hand. A bezel setting around a pear creates a sleek, graphic look that protects the full perimeter. A prong setting with four or five prongs raises the stone higher and allows more light to enter from the sides.

### Why Now
## The Revival

The pear's resurgence has several causes. Celebrities wearing striking pear rings in recent years increased visibility. But the deeper reason is that the shape does something round and oval do not: it has a directionality, an asymmetry, that reads as a choice rather than a default.

For buyers who want an engagement ring that signals consideration rather than convention, the pear offers that without requiring unusual materials or elaborate settings. The shape itself makes the statement.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does a pear diamond face up larger than a round brilliant of the same carat weight?**
Yes, typically. Because of its elongated shape, a pear spreads more surface area face-up than a round of the same weight. A 1.00-carat pear will usually appear slightly larger than a 1.00-carat round when viewed from above on the hand, though the difference depends on the specific proportions of each stone.

**How do I evaluate a pear for bow-tie?**
View the stone in multiple lighting conditions: bright daylight, office fluorescent light, and dim ambient light. Under each condition, look for a dark band running horizontally across the widest part of the stone. A minimal bow-tie is acceptable; a strong, dark bow-tie is not. This assessment must be done in person, not from photos.

**Which direction should the point face when worn?**
Personal preference. Point toward the fingertip is traditional; point toward the hand is increasingly common in contemporary design. Try both orientations with an example piece to see which you prefer on your hand before committing to a setting.

**What is the ideal length-to-width ratio for a pear?**
The most versatile range is 1.50 to 1.65. Below 1.45 the pear reads more like a rounded oval; above 1.75 it becomes very elongated and narrow, which suits some hands and not others. If you can view stones in person, proportions matter more than ratios on paper.

**How should the point of a pear be protected in the setting?**
A V-prong is standard for pear settings because it wraps around and protects the pointed tip from chipping. This is structural, not decorative. A pear set without tip protection in a ring worn daily is at risk of damage over time.
