*Most buyers look at a diamond certificate for four numbers and stop — but the proportions diagram, clarity plot, and comments section contain the real information that separates good stones from great ones within the same grade.*

---

### The Certificate Basics
## What a GIA Report Actually Contains

A GIA (Gemological Institute of America) Diamond Grading Report is a standardized document reporting the results of a professional gemological examination of a specific diamond. The report includes a GIA report number, the date of issue, the shape and cutting style, the measurements in millimeters, and the 4Cs grades — carat weight, color, clarity, and cut (for round brilliants). Beyond those headline items, the report contains a proportions diagram, a clarity plot, a laser inscription number, and additional grading information fields.

The report number is the most critical piece of information for verification. Every certified GIA stone has this number laser-inscribed on its girdle, and the GIA online report check system allows anyone to verify that the number matches the physical stone and retrieve the original grading data. Verifying the inscription against the report before accepting delivery is non-negotiable for any significant purchase.

The measurements field — listed in millimeters as diameter and depth for round stones, or length by width by depth for fancy shapes — tells you the physical dimensions of the stone before any proportional analysis. These numbers allow you to calculate the stone's face-up area and verify that the depth is within efficient proportional ranges for the shape. A one-carat round listed as 6.3 mm rather than 6.5 mm tells you immediately that the stone is cut deep and is sacrificing face-up size for carat weight.

### The Proportions Diagram
## What the Numbers Under the Diamond Drawing Mean

The proportions diagram on a GIA round brilliant report is a cross-section drawing of the stone with all major proportional measurements labeled numerically. Table percentage, crown angle, crown height, girdle thickness range, pavilion angle, pavilion depth, culet size, and total depth percentage all appear here. For buyers who want to evaluate cut quality beyond the summary Cut grade, this is where the real information lives.

Table percentage — the table's width as a percentage of the girdle diameter — should fall between 53 and 62 percent for maximum optical performance in round brilliants. Crown angle — the angle of the main crown facets relative to the girdle plane — should fall between 33.7 and 35.8 degrees. Pavilion angle — the most critical single measurement — should fall between 40.6 and 41.0 degrees for ideal light reflection. These ranges define the "ideal proportions" zone popularized by AGS (American Gem Society) research and refined by GIA's optical modeling work.

A stone can receive a GIA Excellent cut grade while sitting slightly outside the tightest ideal proportions ranges, because the GIA Excellent encompasses a broader range of proportions that all produce high optical performance, not just the absolute narrowest ideal window. The proportions diagram allows buyers who want to verify they are within the tightest ideal range to do so without relying solely on the summary grade.

For fancy shapes — oval, pear, cushion, emerald, and others — GIA does not issue a cut grade, but the measurements are still reported. For fancy shapes, the length-to-width ratio (calculated from the reported measurements) is the primary proportional data point that buyers use to evaluate whether a stone's outline matches their aesthetic preferences. A length-to-width ratio of 1.35 to 1.45 is considered the classic oval proportions; below 1.3 the stone reads as almost round, above 1.5 the stone is very elongated.

### The Clarity Plot
## What the Diagram of Inclusions Tells You

The clarity plot is a schematic diagram showing the position of all inclusions and surface characteristics within the stone. Inclusions are shown by their position in the face-up view (the table diagram) and in a side view (the pavilion diagram). The symbols used in the clarity plot correspond to specific types of characteristics — crystals, clouds, feathers, pinpoints, needles, indented naturals, extra facets, and others — each with its own symbol listed in the legend.

The clarity plot reveals something the grade alone cannot: where the inclusions are. An SI1 clarity grade tells you that inclusions are noticeable at 10x magnification but gives you no information about whether they are centrally located under the table (where they are most visible face-up) or near the girdle (where they are least visible). The plot shows you directly. A cluster of small inclusions near the girdle at the 4 o'clock position in the face-up view is fundamentally different from a large crystal centered under the table, even if both stones receive the same clarity grade.

Reading the plot requires knowing the symbol conventions. A green symbol indicates a surface-reaching blemish; a red symbol indicates an internal characteristic. The plot also shows whether inclusions are light-colored (open symbols) or dark (filled symbols). A dark crystal inclusion under the table creates a visible dark spot in the face-up view at much higher clarity grades than a colorless cloud inclusion in the same position.

### Additional Report Fields
## Comments, Polish, Symmetry, and Fluorescence

Polish and symmetry grades appear separately from the overall cut grade for round brilliants and are the only cut-quality grades for fancy shapes. Both are reported on the same Excellent-to-Poor scale as the overall cut grade. For round brilliants, Polish and Symmetry grades should both be Excellent or Very Good to support the best optical performance. A stone with Excellent cut but Good symmetry has a specific facet alignment deficiency that the cut grade alone does not reveal.

Fluorescence is reported with an intensity grade (None, Faint, Medium, Strong, Very Strong) and a color (almost always Blue). Understanding the fluorescence grade's implication for the specific stone's color grade is one of the most important contextual reads a buyer needs to make. Strong blue fluorescence in a G-I color stone is an asset in outdoor daylight; the same intensity in a D-F stone requires physical examination for potential haziness.

The comments field at the bottom of the report notes anything the grader found noteworthy that does not fit the standard data fields. This can include notation of natural inclusions preserved during cutting, treatment disclosures, or notes about unusual characteristics. Any notation in the comments field should be reviewed and understood before purchase.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Can I trust a GIA report from any dealer?**
You can trust the GIA report if you verify that the stone matches it. The GIA inscription number on the girdle must match the report number, and the GIA report check website will confirm the certificate is in GIA's database. A dealer who refuses to allow this verification is a dealer you should not buy from. The report itself is reliable; the stone being sold with it needs to be confirmed as the same stone.

**What is a clarity plot used for?**
The clarity plot shows you where inclusions are located within the stone, which the grade alone does not tell you. Use it to determine whether the key inclusion driving the clarity grade is centrally located under the table (most visible) or near the girdle and pavilion (least visible). A well-positioned inclusion in a VS2 stone may be completely invisible face-up; a centrally placed inclusion in the same grade may be the first thing you notice.

**Why does the GIA report show a range for girdle thickness?**
The girdle thickness varies around the circumference of a round brilliant because the facets that form the girdle alternate between thinner and thicker zones. The report shows the minimum and maximum thickness encountered during measurement, expressed as the thin-thick range (for example, Thin to Medium). The critical reading is whether either endpoint reaches "Extremely Thin" or "Extremely Thick," both of which are problematic for different reasons.

**What does "laser drilled" mean on a GIA report?**
Laser drilling is a treatment in which a laser beam is used to create a channel through the stone to a dark inclusion, followed by chemical treatment to bleach or dissolve the inclusion. The treatment improves the visual appearance of heavily included stones. GIA discloses laser drilling in the comments and on the clarity grade assessment. Laser drilled stones trade at a significant discount to untreated stones of the same apparent clarity because the treatment is artificial and permanent.

**Should I buy a diamond without a GIA or AGS report?**
Not at any significant price point. Without a report from a trusted laboratory, the grade claims on the stone — color, clarity, cut — are supported only by the seller's word or a report from a laboratory you may not be able to evaluate for reliability. The cost of GIA or AGS certification is a small percentage of any significant diamond purchase and is the most important protection the buyer has. Decline unstated-origin reports; the few dollars the certification costs the seller is not a reason to accept unverified quality claims.
