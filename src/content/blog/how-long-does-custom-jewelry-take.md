*The clients who are happiest with their custom commissions are the ones who started the process with enough time — the ones who are disappointed are almost always the ones who called six weeks before the date they needed the piece.*

### The Stages
## Where the Time Goes in a Custom Commission

A custom jewelry commission passes through five stages before the finished piece reaches the client: design consultation and brief, design development and approval, stone sourcing (when required), fabrication, and final inspection and delivery. Each stage has its own time requirements, and the overall timeline is the sum of them — there is no stage that can be reliably compressed without affecting the quality of the stages that follow it.

Design consultation is typically completed in one to two sessions of an hour each. This covers the brief, reference material review, and initial direction-setting. Design development — from first sketch through client approval — takes one to three weeks for straightforward pieces and three to six weeks for complex, novel designs with multiple iteration rounds. This stage cannot be rushed because the client must have adequate time to review, consider, and respond to each design iteration. A client who approves a design in haste because they feel the pressure of a deadline and then wants to change something after fabrication has begun has created an expensive problem for both parties.

Stone sourcing, when we are finding a stone rather than using existing inventory, takes one to two weeks for common stone types in standard sizes — round brilliant diamonds in G–H/VS–SI, for example. Sourcing unusual stones — a specific Kashmir sapphire, a large fancy-color diamond, a fine Burma ruby — may take longer, sometimes several weeks, because the stone must be located, previewed, graded, and transported. No fabrication begins until the stone is physically in our hands.

### The Fabrication
## What Controls How Long the Bench Work Takes

Fabrication time depends primarily on piece complexity. A simple solitaire ring — one stone, four prongs, a shank — takes three to five business days at the bench. A multi-stone ring with pave detail, gallery work, and a complex setting takes two to three weeks. A full tennis bracelet with matched stones through the complete circumference takes three to four weeks. An articulated flex bracelet with multiple rows and custom hinge engineering takes four to six weeks.

These are bench time estimates — they do not include the queue of commissions ahead of the new piece. A busy atelier carries multiple commissions simultaneously, and a piece that requires four weeks of bench time may take six to eight weeks of calendar time if other work is in front of it. This is normal and not a deficiency of service. A studio that promises everything immediately is either not busy — which is a signal — or is delivering work that was not made fresh for you.

Quality benchmarks also take time. After casting, metal must be finished before setting. Finishing — removing casting marks, achieving the correct surface state, checking all dimensions — is not a fast step. After setting, every prong tip is inspected individually, every stone is tested for security, and the piece is cleaned and polished to its final state. The inspection-to-delivery sequence requires one to two business days minimum. Any jeweler who skips it is skipping the quality confirmation step.

### The Calendar
## How to Plan Your Commission Around a Meaningful Date

The most common mistake in custom jewelry commissioning is starting too close to a meaningful date. Engagements, anniversaries, milestone birthdays, and holiday gifts all have fixed calendar positions, and the production timeline does not compress to accommodate them. A piece that needs to be delivered by December 25 should be commissioned by October 1 at the latest for complex pieces, November 1 for straightforward commissions in a well-managed studio.

The safest commissioning calendar adds a buffer equal to half the estimated production time to the completion date. If a piece takes eight weeks to produce, plan for twelve weeks from the first consultation to have the piece in hand. This buffer absorbs the inevitable — a stone that needs to be reordered, a design iteration that takes longer than expected, a holiday closure that interrupts the fabrication schedule.

Rush commissions are possible for some piece types and some timelines. A simple solitaire pendant sourced from available inventory with a standard setting can be produced in two to three weeks. A complex engagement ring designed from scratch with a custom stone cannot be rushed to that timeline without compromising the design, the fabrication, or the stone selection. Be direct about your timeline at the first consultation — an honest jeweler will tell you what is and is not achievable and help you prioritize accordingly.

### What People
## Ask Us

**What is the absolute minimum timeline for a custom engagement ring?**
A genuinely custom engagement ring — custom design, custom stone sourcing, fabrication, and inspection — cannot be responsibly delivered in less than six weeks. A ring using a standard design from our existing repertoire with a stone from current inventory can be completed in three to four weeks. If your proposal date is less than three weeks away and the ring does not yet exist, we need to have an honest conversation about alternatives.

**Can I speed up the process by paying a rush fee?**
Rush fees can accelerate the fabrication queue and, in some cases, the stone sourcing process. They cannot create bench time that does not exist or eliminate the minimum time required for quality work. We can usually accommodate reasonable rush requests with adequate lead time — contact us directly about specific timelines rather than assuming a rush fee solves the problem automatically.

**What happens if the piece isn't ready on time?**
If a commission is delayed beyond the agreed delivery date due to factors on our end — fabrication issues, stone sourcing delays — we communicate that as early as possible and adjust the timeline transparently. We have held commissions because the final inspection identified an issue that needed correction, and we will always choose the delay over delivering a piece that is not right. Clients should expect this standard and plan their calendar accordingly.

**Do I need to be present for all stages of the process?**
The consultation and design approval stages require your input, either in person or via detailed written and visual communication. The fabrication stage does not require your presence, though you are welcome to visit the studio at arranged times. The delivery appointment is where you see the finished piece for the first time, and we strongly prefer this happens in person so we can address any questions immediately.

**Can you commission a piece as a surprise gift without the recipient knowing?**
Yes, this is common. We design around the measurements and style preferences communicated by the person commissioning the piece. For rings, sizing the gift without the recipient's participation requires knowing their ring size, which can sometimes be obtained discreetly through a borrowed ring or a jeweler's sizing consultation presented as something else. We have helped clients navigate this situation many times.
