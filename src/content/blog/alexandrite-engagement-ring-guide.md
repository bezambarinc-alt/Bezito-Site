*Alexandrite is the only major gemstone that changes color under different lighting — and that phenomenon, not its rarity, is why it earns a place in serious jewelry.*

---

### The Phenomenon
## Why Alexandrite Changes Color and What to Look For

The color change in alexandrite comes from an unusual interaction between the stone's chromium content and the way human eyes perceive wavelengths of light. Under daylight or fluorescent light, the stone absorbs red and transmits green. Under incandescent light — candlelight, tungsten bulbs, warm LEDs — it absorbs green and the red passes through. A fine alexandrite reads as a vivid bluish-green in daylight and a deep purplish-red by lamplight. Lesser stones shift from grayish-green to brownish-red. That difference in quality is everything.

The strength of the color change is graded informally across the trade. The best stones show what dealers call a complete change — two distinct, saturated hues with no muddy overlap. A stone that shifts only partially, or that lands in dull, desaturated territory at either end, is considered weak. When buying alexandrite for an engagement ring, test the stone under both cool and warm light sources before committing. A jeweler who refuses that test is not a jeweler you should trust.

Natural alexandrite from the original Russian deposits — the Ural Mountains, worked out by the mid-twentieth century — commands premiums that rival fine rubies and sapphires. Modern supply comes primarily from Brazil, Sri Lanka, and East Africa. Brazilian material is plentiful but often shows incomplete color change. Sri Lankan stones tend toward a more elegant green-to-red shift. The Heisei-period discoveries in Madagascar produced stones competitive with the best Russian material.

### The Stone's Durability
## How Alexandrite Holds Up as a Daily-Wear Ring

Alexandrite is a variety of chrysoberyl, the third-hardest natural gemstone after diamond and corundum (sapphire and ruby). It registers 8.5 on the Mohs hardness scale, which means it resists scratching from virtually every material you will encounter in daily life. It has no cleavage — no structural weak plane that a sharp impact will split along. That combination of hardness and toughness makes alexandrite one of the most practical colored stones for a ring worn every day.

The stone requires no special maintenance beyond the same care you would give any fine gem. Ultrasonic cleaning is generally safe for untreated alexandrite, though if there is any uncertainty about the stone's origin or treatment history, use warm soapy water and a soft brush. Most reputable dealers will disclose whether a stone has been treated. Untreated natural alexandrite carries significant value; lab-grown alexandrite is widely available and affordable but trades the rarity premium for accessibility.

### Setting and Metal Choices
## Building a Ring That Serves the Stone

Alexandrite's two-color nature creates an unusual design challenge: the setting must not fight either expression. White gold and platinum are neutral partners that let both the green and red read clearly. Yellow gold can warm the daylight face in a way that feels slightly muddy on some stones; on others, the contrast is deliberate and striking. Rose gold tends to compete with the red phase. My recommendation is platinum or high-alloy white gold for the primary metal, particularly if the stone's green phase is its stronger color.

The mount itself should be simple. Alexandrite's color change is the drama — the setting should frame it without adding visual noise. A four- or six-prong solitaire, a bezel that shows enough of the girdle to let light enter freely from the sides, or a low-profile halo in pavé-set diamonds all serve the stone without distracting from it. Elaborate multi-metal split-shank designs work against alexandrite specifically because the stone already contains its own contrast.

For center-stone size, alexandrite commands its best prices in the two-to-three carat range, where the color change is vivid and the face-up surface is large enough to appreciate the full shift. Stones under one carat are more accessible in price, but the color transition can look subtle in smaller faces. If budget is the constraint, a well-cut one-carat stone with a strong change is better than a larger stone with a weak one.

### Origins and Pricing
## What Fine Alexandrite Actually Costs

Certified natural alexandrite with strong color change sells for between $5,000 and $25,000 per carat in the commercial-to-fine range. Exceptional Russian or Malagasy stones with complete, vivid change and no visible inclusions have sold for north of $60,000 per carat at auction. These are not commodity prices — they reflect genuine scarcity in a stone that has no industrial substitute.

Lab-grown alexandrite is available for a fraction of those prices and produces the same optical phenomenon through the same chemical mechanism. The color change in lab-grown stones can be exceptional — sometimes more complete than natural material in the same price range. For clients who want the color-change experience without the rarity premium, lab-grown alexandrite is a completely legitimate choice. The decision comes down to whether you are buying an heirloom asset or a beautiful object. Both answers are valid; only one requires the natural stone.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Is alexandrite a good choice for an engagement ring?**
Alexandrite is an excellent choice if the wearer understands what they are getting: a stone that looks completely different under different lighting conditions. Its hardness and toughness are better than most colored stones. The main consideration is price — fine natural alexandrite is significantly more expensive than a comparable sapphire.

**What causes alexandrite to change color?**
The color change is caused by chromium in the crystal structure. Chromium absorbs specific wavelengths of light, and the balance shifts between the red and green portions of the spectrum depending on the light source's composition. Daylight contains more blue-green wavelengths; incandescent light contains more red. The eye interprets the dominant transmitted wavelength as the stone's color.

**How do I know if an alexandrite is natural or lab-grown?**
A reputable gemological laboratory — GIA or AGL (American Gemological Laboratories) — can determine natural versus synthetic origin through spectroscopic testing. The certificate will state origin explicitly. Any dealer who cannot provide or obtain laboratory documentation for a natural alexandrite is not a dealer you should buy from at fine-stone prices.

**What is the best color-change percentage to look for?**
Strong alexandrite shows what the trade calls a complete change — typically described as showing 75 to 100 percent shift between the two color expressions. Anything below 50 percent is considered weak and significantly reduces both visual appeal and resale value. Ask the seller to state the color-change percentage directly and verify it yourself under both light sources in the store.

**Can alexandrite be set in yellow gold?**
It can, and some designers use yellow gold deliberately to warm the red phase. The risk is that yellow gold can muddy the green phase in daylight on certain stones. The safest approach is to test your specific stone against yellow and white metal before deciding. If the green face looks olive or gray against yellow gold, switch to platinum.
