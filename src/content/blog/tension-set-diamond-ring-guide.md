*A tension set diamond doesn't just sit in a ring — it's held there by physics, precision, and a tolerance measured in thousandths of an inch.*

---

### What It Is
## The Tension Setting Explained

A tension setting holds a diamond between two metal walls using the spring force of the shank itself. There are no prongs. There is no bezel. The stone appears to float, suspended mid-air between two edges of metal, with light pouring through from every direction. That floating effect is not a design trick. It is the direct result of compressive force — the shank is engineered to grip the stone under constant lateral pressure, and that grip is what keeps the diamond secure.

The metal used matters enormously. Platinum and high-karat gold alloys have the elasticity to hold tension without fatiguing over years of wear. Titanium and cobalt chrome are also used, though they require specialized tooling. The shank is cut to a width slightly narrower than the diamond's girdle, then the stone is pressed into place. When set correctly, the diamond will not rock, shift, or loosen under normal wear. When set incorrectly, it will.

### Precision First
## Why the Tolerances Are So Demanding

Every tension setting lives or dies by its calibration. The channel cut into the metal must match the stone's girdle thickness within a fraction of a millimeter. Too shallow and the stone will pop free under impact. Too deep and the metal walls close around the girdle, crushing it — a crack that may not appear immediately but will propagate under wear.

This is why tension settings cannot be resized the way a standard prong ring can. The shank's geometry is calibrated to a specific stone. Change the ring's circumference and you change the spring tension. Resize it enough and you release the stone. Reputable jewelers who work in tension settings will tell you upfront: size carefully before you set. The stone goes in last, after every other adjustment has been made.

The cutting of the stone also carries more weight here than in most other setting styles. A round brilliant or princess cut with a slightly irregular girdle — a variation that would go unnoticed under four prongs — becomes a liability in a tension setting. The girdle must be consistent in thickness all the way around. This is one reason why tension set rings demand stones with more rigorous quality control at the cutting stage.

### Light and Drama
## How the Open Setting Changes the Stone

The aesthetic payoff of a tension setting is maximized light return. A prong-set stone still has metal at the girdle and, depending on the design, at or near the culet. A bezel wraps the stone's perimeter. A tension setting exposes the entire pavilion — the lower half of the diamond — to open air and ambient light. The stone collects light from below as well as above, and the result is a livelier, brighter appearance than the same stone would project in a closed mounting.

For fancy cuts — princess, oval, pear — the tension setting emphasizes the stone's silhouette without any metal competing for the eye's attention. The diamond reads as a pure shape suspended in space. That drama is the reason tension settings have been sought by clients who want a ring that is architecturally distinct, not merely decorated.

### Wear and Care
## What Owners Need to Know

A well-made tension setting worn by a careful owner is durable. A tension setting worn during impact sports, heavy manual work, or regular exposure to hard surfaces is not. This is not a weakness unique to tension settings — any open setting is vulnerable to the same conditions — but the margin for error is smaller here because there is no prong to bend back into place. If a tension-set stone is ever displaced by a hard impact, the repair requires a skilled bench jeweler with experience in tension work. It is not a repair that can be done at every shop.

Annual inspection is non-negotiable. A jeweler with the right equipment can check the spring tension, inspect the channel edges for wear, and confirm the stone's movement tolerance before any problem becomes a loss. Clients who do this consistently keep their tension set rings for decades without incident.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Can a tension set ring be resized?**
Resizing a tension setting is possible but significantly more complex than resizing a standard ring. The spring force in the shank is calibrated to the stone's girdle, so any change in diameter affects the holding pressure. A skilled bench jeweler can sometimes add or remove material in a way that preserves the calibration, but the stone must be removed first and reset afterward. The work is time-intensive and not all jewelers will take it on.

**Is a tension set diamond more likely to fall out?**
A properly made and properly maintained tension setting is no more prone to stone loss than a well-made prong setting. The risk comes from poor initial calibration, impact damage that spreads the shank, or skipping annual inspections. The setting style is not inherently fragile — it is unforgiving of shortcuts.

**What diamond shapes work best in a tension setting?**
Round brilliants and princess cuts are the most common choices because their girdles are consistent and predictable. Ovals, pears, and cushions can be tension set but require more precise stone selection since any irregularity in the girdle becomes a fitting problem. Marquise cuts are rarely tension set because the pointed ends introduce stress concentration in the metal.

**Can any diamond be tension set, or does the quality have to be higher?**
The cut quality standard is stricter. Girdle thickness must be consistent all the way around the stone, and any chips or abrasions on the girdle are disqualifying — they become pressure points under the metal's grip. A diamond that would be fine in a prong setting may not meet the standard for a tension setting.

**How do I clean a tension set ring?**
Warm water and a soft brush work well. The open setting actually makes cleaning easier than a prong or bezel setting because there is no cavity beneath the stone where debris can accumulate. Ultrasonic cleaners are generally safe for the metal but should only be used if the stone is confirmed secure — never use an ultrasonic on a ring that is due for inspection.
