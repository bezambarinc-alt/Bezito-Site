*The impulse to upgrade is common, and not always wrong — but the clients who are most satisfied with their upgrade decision are the ones who had a specific, articulable reason that was not "bigger."*

---

### The Good Reasons
## Structural, Personal, and Life-Stage Arguments for an Upgrade

Some engagement rings genuinely should be upgraded, and not for reasons of status or size. A ring that no longer fits because of a life change — pregnancy, health conditions, significant weight change — may be a candidate for a ring that accommodates the new finger size or a different shank profile. A ring with structural wear that makes stone security a genuine concern is a ring that needs either repair or replacement; if the repair cost approaches the design value of the piece, replacement with an upgraded design is a rational choice.

A ring originally purchased at a budget that was the right decision at the time, but that no longer represents the couple's current circumstances, is a legitimate upgrade candidate. Many engagements happen when both parties are at the beginning of their careers. A ring chosen at that moment is appropriate to that moment. Ten or fifteen years later, upgrading the ring is not an act of dissatisfaction with the original commitment — it is an acknowledgment that the material expression of that commitment can grow with the relationship.

Design evolution is another valid reason. Fine jewelry design has changed meaningfully over the past twenty years, and a ring purchased in 2004 may reflect 2004 aesthetics in ways that neither the wearer nor their current sensibility would choose today. Upgrading to a design that reflects who the person is now rather than who they were then is a legitimate personal choice.

### The Wrong Reasons
## Why Chasing a Larger Stone or a Trend Is Usually a Disappointment

The most common poor reason for a ring upgrade is wanting a larger center stone because of comparison to someone else's ring. This is worth naming directly because it is the driver behind more upgrade conversations than any other single motivation, and it consistently produces clients who are satisfied for a short period and then unsatisfied again as the next comparison arrives. A ring that was chosen for size-relative-to-peers will always be vulnerable to the next peer's larger stone.

A related poor reason is upgrading to chase a current trend in ring design. Solitaire rings, halo rings, three-stone rings, and east-west settings have each cycled through peak popularity periods in the past twenty years. A ring upgraded to match the current trend is a ring that will read as dated at the next trend cycle. Upgrades motivated by trend-chasing tend to produce pieces that the wearer is equally restless about in five years. An upgrade driven by personal aesthetic conviction — this is the ring I would choose if I were choosing today — tends to produce pieces the client wears with lasting satisfaction.

The financial logic of upgrades also deserves honest treatment. Fine diamond jewelry does not appreciate in value the way many clients expect. The secondary market for used fine jewelry prices below the original retail cost. The stone from an existing ring, traded toward an upgrade, will be credited at secondary market value rather than its original retail cost. This means the financial exchange in an upgrade is almost always less favorable than the client anticipates. Knowing this in advance prevents the upgrade from feeling like an unwelcome financial surprise.

### The Process
## How to Do an Upgrade Correctly If You Decide to Proceed

An upgrade should begin with a design consultation, not a stone selection. The right order of operations is to identify what specifically you want to be different about the new ring — design, stone size, stone shape, metal, all of the above — and then design around that clarity. Clients who walk into an upgrade conversation knowing only that they want something "better" end up in an open-ended process that is harder to bring to a satisfying conclusion than clients who can say "I want an oval stone in a minimal bezel setting in 18k yellow gold."

If the existing ring contains a significant stone that you want to keep, have it graded independently before the upgrade conversation begins. Know what you have. The stone's current grade, weight, and condition determine its trade-in credit and inform whether the stone itself should remain in the upgrade or be replaced along with the design.

Consider keeping the original ring rather than trading it. A ring that was chosen at the beginning of a marriage is an object with a meaning that its trade-in credit does not capture. Some clients keep the original ring in a safe deposit box after upgrading — it is not worn, but it exists. Others have the original stone reset into a pendant or a right-hand ring so the stone continues to be worn in a new context. These options deserve serious consideration before the original piece is traded.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Do most people who upgrade their engagement ring keep the original stone?**
Roughly half of our upgrade clients keep the original stone in the new design or in a secondary piece. The other half prefer to start completely fresh with a new stone and sell or retain the original ring separately. There is no right answer — it depends on the sentimental value of the original stone relative to the couple's vision for the new piece.

**How much credit will I receive for my original ring's diamond?**
Diamond trade-in credit is calculated at the secondary market value of the stone, which varies by size, quality, and current market conditions. As a general range, trade-in credit typically amounts to 40 to 60 percent of the stone's original retail price for diamonds in good condition with documentation. Stones without documentation, or in lower-demand size and quality ranges, receive less credit. We provide a written trade-in assessment before any upgrade commitment.

**Should both partners be involved in the upgrade decision?**
Yes, more so than in the original engagement ring purchase. The original engagement ring is typically a surprise. An upgrade is a joint adult decision with financial implications for both parties. The design preferences and financial parameters of both partners should drive the upgrade process. An upgrade that one partner wants and the other is ambivalent about is not a good starting position.

**Is there a right time in a marriage to upgrade an engagement ring?**
Significant anniversaries — five years, ten years, twenty-five years — are common triggers, partly because they represent natural milestone moments and partly because they coincide with meaningful changes in the couple's circumstances. There is no wrong time, and there is no obligation. An upgrade should be initiated because it is genuinely wanted, not because an anniversary creates an expected opportunity.

**Can you work with a ring from another jeweler as the starting point for an upgrade?**
Yes. We evaluate any ring or stone brought to us for an upgrade consultation regardless of its origin. We will provide a frank assessment of the stone's quality and trade-in value, the existing ring's design characteristics, and the options for the new piece. We do not require that the original piece came from us to provide full upgrade service.
