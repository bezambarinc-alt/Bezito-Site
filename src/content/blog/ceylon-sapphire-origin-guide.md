*Sri Lanka has been mining sapphires since before the Roman Empire traded for them — the geological continuity of that production is one of the reasons the material remains the benchmark for fine blue sapphire.*

---

### What Ceylon Origin Means
## The Geology Behind the Color

"Ceylon" is the former colonial name for Sri Lanka, and it remains the trade designation for sapphires from that island's gem-producing regions — principally the Ratnapura district in the Sabaragamuwa Province, known historically as the City of Gems. The sapphires emerge from secondary deposits where billions of years of erosion have concentrated the corundum from its original host rock into river gravels and alluvial sediments. Miners work these gravels with methods that have changed relatively little over centuries.

The geological character of Sri Lankan corundum produces several consistent optical signatures. The stones tend toward lighter tone compared to Thai or Australian sapphire — a medium to medium-dark blue rather than the near-black of material from some other origins. The color is often described as "cornflower blue," a pure, slightly violet-tinged blue with high clarity and a particular luminosity under natural light. The combination of tone, saturation, and transparency gives Ceylon sapphires a liveliness that is distinct from the denser, heavier appearance of deeply colored material from other origins.

### The Cornflower Blue Benchmark
## A Color Designation That Actually Means Something

The trade uses "cornflower blue" specifically and intentionally. The flower — Centaurea cyanus — produces a medium-blue with a slight violet component that is immediately recognizable. The color in sapphire that earns this designation is not the darkest possible blue, nor the lightest — it occupies a specific saturation and tone range that is most commonly achieved in Ceylon material. Some Sri Lankan sapphires are darker, and some are lighter, and the quality distribution is wide, but the classic cornflower blue exists predominantly in Sri Lankan origin.

Kashmir sapphires — the rarest and most expensive of all — share some of the lightness and violet component of Ceylon material but add a velvety, diffuse quality from microscopic inclusions that scatter light internally. Kashmir material has been essentially exhausted since the 1920s, which is why documented Kashmir sapphires trade at auction prices that are multiples of comparable Ceylon stones. Ceylon is the accessible, still-producing alternative to Kashmir for buyers who want the lighter, vivid blue character.

### Origin Documentation
## Why the Report Is Not Optional

Origin determination for sapphire requires sophisticated testing — trace element analysis comparing the stone's chemistry to reference databases of known-origin material, combined with inclusion fingerprinting that identifies the characteristic silk patterns, zoning, and mineral inclusions typical of each deposit. The leading laboratories for this work are Gübelin, SSEF, and AGL, with GIA also performing origin determinations on sapphires.

A Ceylon origin determination from one of these laboratories adds meaningful value to a fine stone and provides the buyer with the documentation needed to substantiate the premium they paid. Without a report, "Ceylon origin" is a claim that cannot be verified and should not be priced at a Ceylon premium. Buying significant natural sapphires without origin documentation from a recognized laboratory is a risk that sophisticated buyers do not take.

### Heat Treatment in Ceylon Sapphire
## The Distinction Between Heated and Unheated Material

The vast majority of Ceylon sapphires available commercially have been heat-treated. Heating improves color saturation, dissolves some silk inclusions that reduce transparency, and produces the vivid, clean blue that the market demands. Heat treatment is accepted as standard in the sapphire trade, and a heated Ceylon sapphire with excellent color and a credible origin report is a legitimate, valuable stone.

Unheated Ceylon sapphires — documented as "no heat" on a laboratory report — command premiums of 30 to 100 percent above comparable heated material. The premium reflects rarity: achieving the full, saturated blue without heat treatment requires starting with rough of exceptional natural color. Unheated material above two carats with fine color is genuinely uncommon, and the no-heat designation is meaningful and verifiable. If a seller claims unheated status without a laboratory report to support it, the claim is worthless.

### What People
## Ask Us

**How does Ceylon sapphire differ from Burma (Mogok) sapphire?**
Burma sapphires from the Mogok Valley tend toward a deeper, more intensely saturated blue with a slight red fluorescence that gives them a velvety warmth under incandescent light. Ceylon sapphires are typically lighter in tone, more cornflower blue, and more transparent. Both are fine sapphires, and the price difference between comparable top-quality Ceylon and Burma material has narrowed — Burma still commands the highest premiums, but the gap varies with individual stones.

**What is a fair price range for fine Ceylon sapphire?**
Quality heated Ceylon sapphire in the one-to-three carat range with good color runs approximately $1,500 to $5,000 per carat at retail. Exceptional color in the three-to-five carat range with a Gübelin or AGL report can reach $8,000 to $20,000 per carat. Unheated material with top color adds 30 to 100 percent above those figures. Large heated stones above five carats with vivid color are priced individually.

**Is Ceylon the same as "Sri Lanka" on a modern report?**
Yes. "Ceylon" is the historical trade name; modern laboratory reports use "Sri Lanka" as the country designation. The two terms refer to the same origin, and either designation on a report from Gübelin, SSEF, AGL, or GIA is equally authoritative.

**How important is the cut in a Ceylon sapphire?**
Very important. Ceylon sapphires are frequently cut to maximize carat weight rather than optical performance, resulting in stones that are too deep or too shallow for the setting. A well-proportioned oval or cushion cut with proper depth percentages will show dramatically better color face-up than a window-y, poorly cut stone. Evaluating cut is part of evaluating the stone.

**What setting metal best complements Ceylon sapphire?**
Platinum and white gold create maximum color contrast, letting the blue read as purely blue without any warmth from the setting. Yellow gold adds a warm undertone that some buyers find complementary and others find distracting. The traditional association of fine sapphires with platinum and white gold persists for visual reasons — the cold metal and the blue stone reinforce each other. The choice is personal, but the platinum pairing has a history and logic behind it.
