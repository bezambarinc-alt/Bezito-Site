### The Origin
## Where These Stones Actually Come From

Diamond forms roughly 100 miles beneath the earth's surface under intense pressure and heat, then travels upward through volcanic pipes over billions of years. Moissanite — silicon carbide — was first discovered in 1893 inside a meteorite crater in Arizona by Nobel laureate Henri Moissan, who initially mistook the crystals for diamonds. Natural moissanite is so rare it is essentially unavailable commercially. Every moissanite sold today is grown in a laboratory, a process refined since the 1990s to produce stones that are optically near-flawless.

That origin story matters for one reason: moissanite's supply is controlled, consistent, and theoretically unlimited. Diamond supply — particularly high-quality natural diamond — is not.

### The Look
## How Each Stone Handles Light

This is where the conversation gets technical, and where buyers are most frequently misled. Diamond's refractive index — its ability to bend and return light — sits at 2.42. Moissanite's refractive index is 2.65, which is higher. In pure optical terms, moissanite bends more light than diamond. The result is what gemologists call dispersion, the rainbow-colored flashes a stone throws when it moves. Diamond's dispersion coefficient is 0.044. Moissanite's is 0.104 — more than twice as high.

Under direct sunlight or stage lighting, a moissanite will broadcast more colored fire than a diamond of identical cut and size. Some buyers love this. Others find it excessive, particularly in larger stones above two carats. A diamond's light return tends to read as white brilliance, which many people associate with the look of a fine engagement ring. Neither is wrong. They are genuinely different optical characters, and you should see both in person before deciding.

### The Hardness
## Durability Over a Lifetime of Wear

Diamond is the hardest natural material on earth, rating 10 on the Mohs hardness scale. Moissanite rates 9.25 — harder than sapphire, harder than ruby, harder than every other gemstone used in jewelry except diamond. For practical wear, the difference is negligible. Neither stone will scratch from everyday contact. A moissanite will not cloud, chip, or fade over decades of daily wear under normal conditions. The durability argument for choosing diamond over moissanite is largely theoretical for most wearers.

### The Price
## What You Pay and What You Get

A one-carat round brilliant diamond of good quality — G color, VS2 clarity — retails between $4,500 and $7,000 depending on cut quality and where you buy it. A comparable moissanite, meaning the same diameter and similar optical grade, retails between $400 and $700. The price gap is not 10 percent. It is roughly 85 to 90 percent. For buyers who want maximum size and presence within a fixed budget, moissanite is the rational choice. For buyers who want a natural stone with geological history and long-term resale behavior that tracks the diamond market, the math points the other way.

Resale is worth addressing plainly. Neither stone holds value the way financial advisors wish jewelry did. Diamond resale typically returns 20 to 40 cents on the retail dollar. Moissanite resale is minimal — the secondary market for lab-grown silicon carbide is thin. If resale value is a meaningful factor in your decision, that belongs in the conversation up front.

### The Setting
## Why Craftsmanship Closes the Gap

The most consequential decision in any engagement ring is not the stone. It is the metalwork holding that stone for the next 50 years. A poorly set diamond will look worse than a masterfully set moissanite. The reverse is equally true. A well-engineered prong setting — one built to hold tension correctly, sized precisely to the girdle of the stone, finished so no metal obscures the pavilion — makes a moissanite read with more dignity than a sloppy diamond mount will ever achieve. After four decades of working with both materials, this is the observation I return to most often: buyers agonize over the stone and accept the setting, when the ratio of attention should be reversed.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Does moissanite test as diamond on a standard diamond tester?**
Early diamond testers measured thermal conductivity, and moissanite fooled them because its thermal signature is close to diamond's. Modern testers use electrical conductivity in combination with thermal readings and will correctly identify moissanite. Any reputable jeweler working today will have equipment that distinguishes the two immediately.

**Can a trained jeweler tell moissanite from diamond by looking at it?**
Yes. Under 10x magnification, moissanite shows characteristic doubling of facet junctions — a visual artifact of its double refractive index — that diamond does not produce. To the naked eye at arm's length, the distinction requires experience and ideal lighting conditions to catch reliably.

**Does moissanite hold its color over time?**
Charles and Colvard, the primary manufacturer of gem-grade moissanite, has been producing stones since 1998 and backs them with a limited lifetime warranty covering color and clarity changes. The stones have not shown degradation under normal wear. Current colorless grades (DEF equivalent) have been stable in independent testing.

**Is a moissanite engagement ring considered less meaningful?**
That is a social question, not a gemological one. The meaning of an engagement ring comes from the commitment it represents and the care taken in choosing and making it, not from the mineral composition of the center stone. Couples who understand what they are choosing and choose deliberately do not report regret.

**What size moissanite would you recommend for a classic solitaire?**
For a round solitaire in a four-prong or six-prong setting, stones between 6.5mm and 8mm diameter — roughly one to two carats by diamond weight equivalent — read beautifully without triggering the excessive disco-ball dispersion that larger moissanites can produce under bright light. Above 9mm, I would look at the stone under several different lighting conditions before committing.
