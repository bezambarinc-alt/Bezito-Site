---

For decades, the Argyle mine in the Kimberley region of Western Australia produced approximately 90 percent of the world's pink diamond supply. The stones were typically small, but the mine's volume sustained a category of fine jewelry that would otherwise have been purely the province of auction houses and major collectors.

When Rio Tinto closed the mine in October 2020 after the ore body was exhausted, the supply of new natural pink diamonds dropped substantially. Prices have responded accordingly. For buyers approaching this category now, understanding what has changed and what the stone's actual qualities are, beyond the color and the story, is essential.

### The Color
## and Its Grades

GIA grades pink diamonds on the same colored diamond intensity scale used for all fancy color stones: Faint Pink, Very Light Pink, Light Pink, Fancy Light Pink, Fancy Pink, Fancy Intense Pink, Fancy Vivid Pink, Fancy Deep Pink, and Fancy Purplish Pink as a common hue variant.

The distinction between Fancy Light and Fancy Pink is meaningful on paper and significant in value. The distinction between Fancy Intense and Fancy Vivid is dramatic both visually and in terms of price. A 1.00-carat Fancy Vivid pink diamond, if one appeared at auction in clean clarity, could approach seven figures.

For most buyers, the accessible range is Fancy Light to Fancy Pink. At these intensity levels, the pink is real and visible but not electric. The stones read as warm, feminine, and distinctive without the fire of the finest Argyle stones that appear in major auction catalogs.

A secondary consideration is the hue modifier. Pure pink is the most valuable. Pinkish-brown and brownish-pink are significantly less valuable. Purplish-pink, sometimes called lilac or lavender, is more valuable than brownish-pink. The full GIA color description specifies the modifier.

### Natural vs Lab-Grown
## Pink Diamonds

Lab-grown pink diamonds are now commercially available at prices that represent a fraction of natural stones. A 1.00-carat lab-grown Fancy Intense pink might be priced at $2,000 to $5,000. The equivalent natural stone would be $80,000 to $300,000 or more.

These are not the same product, and the market does not treat them as such. Lab-grown diamonds carry significantly less market value than natural diamonds at resale. For buyers who want the pink diamond aesthetic and are not concerned with natural origin or investment value, lab-grown stones are a legitimate option. For buyers who want a natural pink diamond, the premium for natural origin is substantial and the documentation from GIA is essential.

### The Rarity
## Argument

The common presentation of pink diamonds as romantic or feminine is accurate about the color but misses the more compelling reason to own one: they are genuinely, extraordinarily rare, and that rarity is now compounding.

With the Argyle mine closed, the primary new supply is gone. Pink diamonds still emerge occasionally from other sources, including India, Brazil, and Russia, but at tiny volumes compared to what Argyle produced. The stones that come to market increasingly come from existing collections and estates.

This is the actual story of the pink diamond: not a color that happens to be rare, but a geological accident that no longer replenishes its supply. Whether that matters to the buyer is a personal and financial decision, but it is the honest description of what the market is responding to.

### What People
## Ask Us

**Why did pink diamond prices rise after the Argyle mine closed?**
The Argyle mine supplied approximately 90% of the world's natural pink diamond production. When it closed in 2020, the primary source of new natural pink diamonds was removed from the market. Demand has not decreased, so prices for existing stones have risen substantially. This is straightforward supply reduction without equivalent demand reduction.

**What is the difference between a natural pink diamond and a lab-grown pink diamond?**
Natural pink diamonds formed over billions of years under specific geological conditions in the earth's mantle. Lab-grown diamonds are produced in a controlled environment in weeks using high-pressure, high-temperature or chemical vapor deposition methods. Both are chemically identical. The market treats them very differently: natural stones are investment-grade assets; lab-grown stones depreciate significantly from their retail price.

**Are pink diamonds appropriate for daily-wear engagement rings?**
Yes, as a material. Diamond is 10 on the Mohs scale, and that hardness applies regardless of color. The considerations are the same as any fine diamond: the setting needs to protect the stone appropriately, and the ring should be inspected periodically for prong wear.

**What color within the pink grade is most valuable?**
Pure pink without modifiers is most valuable. Purplish-pink has its own market and is less penalized than brownish-pink. Brownish-pink represents the most significant discount within the pink category. At Fancy Vivid intensity, pure pink is among the most expensive gemstones per carat in the world.

**Should I buy a pink diamond for investment?**
Natural pink diamonds at Fancy Intense and Vivid grades have appreciated significantly, particularly post-Argyle closure. But they are illiquid assets requiring specialized buyers and proper documentation. Investment in colored diamonds should be accompanied by GIA certification, expert appraisal, and realistic expectations about the specialized nature of the resale market.
