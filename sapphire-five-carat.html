A Note from Bez Ambar

On Sapphires

On the Stone Itself

Sapphire is corundum — crystalline aluminum oxide, second only to diamond in hardness on the Mohs scale. The blues come from a trace of iron and titanium swapping into the crystal lattice; chromium turns the same mineral red and gives us ruby. The most coveted deposits sit in a narrow geography: Sri Lanka (Ceylon), Madagascar, Myanmar (Burma), and historically Kashmir. Each origin leaves a different fingerprint inside the stone, and the major laboratories — C. Dunaigre, AGL, Gübelin, SSEF — can read those fingerprints to confirm where a sapphire was born. “Royal Blue” and “Vivid Blue” are not poetry; they are formal colour descriptors used by these laboratories to mark the top of the hue scale.

On Heated and Unheated

Almost every fine sapphire on the market today has been heated. The practice goes back centuries, was industrialised in the 1970s, and is fully accepted by the trade so long as it is disclosed on the lab report. Carefully controlled heating — typically between 800 and 1,800 °C — dissolves microscopic rutile inclusions (“silk”) and coaxes the stone toward a cleaner, more saturated colour. The result is permanent. An unheated stone is one the earth finished on its own, and the laboratory can prove no heat was ever applied. These are the exception, not the rule, and they command a meaningful premium — often a multiple of a comparable heated stone — because nature rarely delivers that combination of colour, clarity, and origin without help. On every report you will see in this collection, the laboratory states the finding plainly: indications of heating or no indications of heating. There is no ambiguity, and we do not edit it.

On Colour & the Mounting

A stone does not live in isolation. The metal it sits in changes how the eye reads it. Platinum and white gold are neutral — they show a sapphire as the laboratory saw it. Yellow gold reflects warm light back through the pavilion and adds saturation; a closed yellow-gold cup behind the stone amplifies the effect further still. For any mounting concept we propose, we can measure the shift before you commit to it — sampling the stone’s body colour in CIE L*a*b* (the same colour space the laboratories use) and quoting the change on the b\* axis, the yellow-to-blue axis on the lab reports. A small Δb\* is a quiet warmth. A large Δb\* is a different stone. Either can be the right answer — but it should be a choice, measured rather than asserted.
